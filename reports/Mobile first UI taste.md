# Win the 12-degree glance without shouting

Mobile-first is **mandatory as a completeness rule and optional as a design order**. Since **July 5, 2024**, Google crawls and indexes with a smartphone crawler only, so anything missing from the phone layout is missing from search. The first touch of a link from social apps, chat or email usually lands on a phone. But device share depends on the task: phones bring about **70% of visits** but desktop holds **47% of time spent**, and B2B SaaS and developer audiences are still mostly desktop. A taste skill should therefore make every page *complete and excellent* at 390 px without making the phone composition the default design priority. For AI-built pages the more common failure is the reverse: a phone column stretched across a 1440 px screen. The phone the user holds is a screen about **12° wide and 25° tall at 32 cm**. Its whole width falls inside the parafovea, so the viewer takes in the entire layout at once and must still make several fixations to read one line. That geometry explains most of what works. Attention comes from **one sharpened element** in the centre band. Google's Material 3 Expressive research reports key elements found **up to 4× faster**, though that figure is company-reported. Ambient motion, auto-carousels and scroll tricks are tuned out or make people sick. Uniqueness belongs in the surface: display type, one image or icon language, one motion signature, copy voice. The control layer stays standard, and Apple's own Liquid Glass shows what happens when novelty reaches the controls. Eye comfort comes from ≥16 px body text, dark-on-light by default, contrast headroom, low-chroma text and calm motion, not from blue-light palettes. Read the confidence of all this with one caveat: most primary pages (NN/g, W3C, Google Search Central, Baymard, UXmatters, NCBI) were blocked in this session and were read through search-engine extracts. Only Apple's and Android's developer docs were fetched directly. The standards numbers are solid; many practitioner percentages need a re-check before anyone quotes them.

Evidence tags used throughout: **[M]** measured (study, log or panel data); **[S]** a normative standard or official platform guideline; **[C]** practitioner consensus (NN/g, Baymard and similar, built on their own testing); **[O]** a single practitioner's or vendor's opinion; **[D]** derived arithmetic from cited numbers; **[I]** this report's inference; **[R]** a first-hand result from this repo. A **(via extract)** marker means the claim was read from a search summary, not the page itself.

## Mobile-first is mandatory for content, optional for composition

The traffic numbers support "phones first" only on average. StatCounter put worldwide page views at **58.99% mobile, 39.26% desktop and 1.76% tablet** in September 2026, but the same series read 52.23% mobile in June. The month-to-month swing is large enough that a methodology change cannot be ruled out **[M]** ([StatCounter](https://gs.statcounter.com/platform-market-share/desktop-mobile-tablet/worldwide), via extract). Contentsquare's Q4 2025 benchmark covers 99 billion sessions. It found **69.9% of website traffic on mobile**, with desktop at about 30% of visits but **47% of total time spent**, and desktop converting at **3.4% against 2% on mobile** **[M]** ([Contentsquare](https://contentsquare.com/guides/digital-experience-benchmark/engagement/)). The split is driven by task. Siege Media's 13.4-billion-session study found mobile share ranging from **about 89% down to about 21%** by industry, and argues that what the visitor is trying to do matters more than who they are **[M]** ([Siege Media](https://www.siegemedia.com/research/mobile-vs-desktop-traffic-share), via extract). Retail runs about **77% mobile** ([Contentsquare retail](https://contentsquare.com/guides/retail-digital-experience/)). Estimates for B2B SaaS and software conflict, at 23.6% (Siege), 34.7% (Similarweb via [Quantumrun](https://www.quantumrun.com/consulting/mobile-website-traffic/)) and 46.1% ([MangoThrive](https://mangothrive.com/mobile-vs-desktop-website-traffic/)) mobile. The defensible range is **25–45% mobile**. The conversion gap itself is contested. Dynamic Yield's trailing year to September 2026 shows mobile ahead (2.86% against 2.23%), and one analysis attributes this to sector mix, since beauty is both the best-converting sector and about 91% mobile **[M/O]** ([RetentionEdge](https://retentionedge.co/articles/average-mobile-ecommerce-conversion-rate)). The reading that fits all of these is that **the phone makes the first impression and the desktop does the deep evaluation** **[I]**.

What is genuinely mandatory is narrower and stricter. Google declared mobile-first indexing complete in October 2023. After **July 5, 2024** the last holdouts were crawled by Googlebot Smartphone only, and "if your site's content is not accessible at all with a mobile device, it will no longer be indexable" **[S]** ([Ahrefs relaying Google](https://ahrefs.com/blog/mobile-first-indexing/)). The requirement is **content parity**: the same text, headings, image alt text, links and structured data. Content inside accordions or tabs still counts if it is in the mobile DOM. Content removed at small widths does not exist for ranking **[S]** ([Shopify dev docs](https://shopify.dev/docs/storefronts/themes/best-practices/performance/ensure-content-parity-mobile-desktop.md)). Google retired the Mobile-Friendly Test in December 2023 and now points to Lighthouse ([Search Engine Land](https://searchengineland.com/google-officially-drops-mobile-usability-report-mobile-friendly-test-tool-and-mobile-friendly-test-api-435377)). Nothing in Google's guidance rewards a design for being *conceived* on a phone. It rewards a page that is complete and works on one. WCAG adds the other hard floor: SC 1.4.10 Reflow forbids two-dimensional scrolling at **320 CSS px**, and SC 1.4.4 requires text to survive 200% resizing **[S]** ([WCAG 2.2](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/#resize-text)).

"Mobile first" has meant three different things since Luke Wroblewski's 2011 book. Its three arguments were growth, constraints and capabilities ([LukeW](https://lukew.com/resources/mobile_first.asp)). The useful part of that original idea is a **priority discipline**: decide the one thing that matters as if you had only 360 px, which is Zeldman's "content first" ([Zeldman](https://zeldman.com/category/content-first/)). The second meaning is a **CSS authoring order**: unprefixed base styles for narrow screens, with `min-width` breakpoints adding complexity. Tailwind bakes this in, with breakpoints at 640/768/1024/1280/1536 px ([Tailwind](https://tailwindcss.com/docs/responsive-design)). The third is **intrinsic layout** in Jen Simmons's sense: fluid columns and rows, `clamp()` type, and components that respond to their container ([Adactio notes](https://adactio.medium.com/676d2799bd02)). Container queries became Baseline Widely Available on **2025-08-14** ([web.dev](https://web.dev/blog/baseline-digest-aug-2025)). The first and third are worth doing on every project, and the second costs nothing. Designing the phone composition before the desktop one is optional. For desktop-majority products it can produce worse desktop layouts **[I]**.

Each approach has a characteristic failure. **Desktop crammed onto a phone** has old but measured costs. NN/g's 2013 study found phone task success of **64% on mobile-specific sites against 53% on full desktop sites** **[M, dated]** ([MediaPost on NN/g](https://www.mediapost.com/publications/article/110103/report-mobile-web-is-miserable.html)). **Mobile stretched onto desktop** gives a single centred 600 px column floating in whitespace, a hamburger menu at 1440 px, and phone-sized cards in one stack. That is the classic generic AI landing page, and for a taste skill it is the more likely failure, because models default to safe, stacked, centred layouts **[I]**. The fix for both is to treat 390 and 1440 as **two compositions of one content priority**, joined by fluid rules. Neither should be a resize of the other.

| Mode | When | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| Mobile-led | Consumer, retail/DTC, food, local, events, travel; social, SMS or QR traffic; single-action landing pages; above 60% mobile | Compose 390 first; desktop is the enhancement |
| Desktop-led | Dashboards, admin, editors, pro tools, data tables; B2B SaaS and developer-tool sites and docs; keyboard or multi-pane tasks; above 55% desktop | Compose 1280–1440 first, then re-compose for the phone. App screens get a *reduced task set* (status, alerts, approvals, one record), not a reflow |
| Both, equally | Portfolios, mixed-traffic marketing sites, studios, editorial | Sketch 390 and 1440 together; phone wins the first impression, desktop wins the evaluation |
| Always | Every project | Mobile-complete content, narrow-first CSS, fluid type and spacing, no horizontal scroll at 360, verified at phone widths |

The thresholds in this table are this report's inference from the traffic data, not tested cut-offs. Portfolios deserve a note because the owner's own site is one. No reliable recruiter-device dataset exists. LinkedIn's *web* traffic is about **74% desktop**, but app-inclusive usage is majority mobile, so links tapped inside the LinkedIn or X apps open on phones ([SQ Magazine](https://sqmagazine.co.uk/linkedin-mobile-vs-desktop-statistics)). One designer reported "about 70% of my portfolio visits are on desktop", while a creative director in the same thread said they often look only on mobile ([Glassdoor thread](https://www.glassdoor.com/Community/ui-and-ux-designers/do-u-think-its-necessary-to-optimize-ur-portfolio-on-mobile-screens-is-there-any-hiring-manager-really-looking-at-a-candidatea)). Portfolios are bimodal: a phone triage glance, then a desktop deep read. A broken phone first screen kills the second look **[I]**. Real analytics split by device and referrer beat every benchmark here.

## A phone is a 12-degree window held 32 cm from the eye

The best measurement of how people hold phones to read is from 2011. Bababekova and colleagues found a mean working distance of **32.2 cm for web pages** (range 19–60 cm, n = 100) and **36.2 cm for text messages**, against about 40 cm for print. Web text was read at a mean of **0.8 M**, roughly newspaper size. The closer distance raises the demands on focusing and on eye coordination **[M]** ([Bababekova et al. 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21499163/), via extract). A 2023 lifespan study with presbyopes exists but its numbers could not be retrieved ([PMC10096186](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10096186/)). That gap matters, since most adults over 45 need larger text.

Put that distance together with a 6.1-inch phone (about 65 × 141 mm, a 393 CSS px viewport, so **1 CSS px ≈ 0.165 mm**) and the geometry follows **[D]**. The screen subtends about **11.6° × 24.8° at 32 cm** and 10.3° × 22° at 36 cm. Reading research puts the fovea at the central **2°**, about 6–8 characters, and the parafovea out to 5° from fixation **[M]** ([Balota & Rayner via Bournemouth eprint](https://eprints.bournemouth.ac.uk/22094/1/lastreviewerversion.pdf)). At phone distance the foveal disc is about **11 mm, or 68 CSS px**. The 10° parafoveal disc is about **56 mm, or 340 CSS px, nearly the whole viewport width** **[D]**. Users therefore perceive a row's full width and layout in one glance, but need several fixations to read it. Vertically, the screen is about two to two and a half parafoveal discs tall. Two design consequences follow **[I]**. First, a strong size or colour contrast anywhere on the screen is seen instantly, so emphasis is cheap and over-emphasis is costly. Second, a single full-width text column is the natural unit. At 16–17 px it lands at about 35–45 characters per line, and two side-by-side text columns would each drop below a useful line.

Gaze has a home. Steven Hoober's observational work concludes that "people look at the center of the screen" and scroll content into it, and recommends bottom padding so the last item can reach mid-viewport **[C]** ([UXmatters, Part 3](https://www.uxmatters.com/mt/archives/2017/07/design-for-fingers-touch-and-people-part-3.php), via extract). A peer-reviewed mobile eye-tracking study found a **"railroad-like" vertical, column-following search pattern** rather than an F. Convention maps predicted where attention landed first, and visual saliency predicted later selection **[M]** ([SN Computer Science](https://doi.org/10.1007/s42979-020-00166-3)). NN/g still finds the F-pattern on mobile, but as a symptom of unformatted text, not a law of reading ([NN/g](https://nngroup.com/articles/f-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content/)). The practical rule is to left-align, front-load key words, break text with subheads and bold lead-ins, and put the screen's purpose in the **upper-middle to centre band** rather than jammed under the status bar.

Grip and reach are messier than the popular thumb-zone heat map suggests. Hoober's 2013 observation of 1,333 people (780 touching the screen) found **49% one-handed, 36% cradled and 15% two-handed**. Some secondaries say 10% two-handed; the original says 15%. Grips shift constantly within a task **[M, observational, "not scientific" by the author's own description]** ([UXmatters 2013](https://www.uxmatters.com/mt/archives/2013/02/how-do-users-really-hold-mobile-devices.php)). The widely repeated "75% of interactions are thumb-driven" counts cradled users tapping with the other thumb, so it is not "75% hold one-handed". Addy Osmani advises against treating the thumb-zone chart as a layout rule ([Osmani](https://addyosmani.com/blog/touch-friendly-design/)). The robust finding is about accuracy. Hoober reports the target size needed for 95% of taps as about **7 mm at the centre, 9 mm along the edges and 12 mm in the corners**. Smashing gives 11 mm top and 12 mm bottom, which conflicts on the edge value ([Hoober](https://medium.com/@shoobe01/44-px-why-apple-is-wrong-6bd6f6846871); [Smashing](https://www.smashingmagazine.com/2023/04/accessible-tap-target-sizes-rage-taps-clicks?ref=refind)). Avrahami's CHI 2015 study found **no** error difference by distance from the edge but strong effects of target size **[M]** ([ACM](https://dl.acm.org/doi/pdf/10.1145/2702123.2702439?download=true)). So size matters more reliably than position. Parhi and colleagues found one-thumb error rates flatten at about **9.2–9.6 mm** **[M]** ([Parhi et al. 2006](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2006/01/parhi-mobileHCI06.pdf)).

Converted to phone CSS pixels, those millimetres put 7 mm at about **42 px**, 9.2–9.6 mm at **55–58 px** and 12 mm at about **73 px** **[D]**. Smashing's "7 mm = 27 px" uses the desktop reference pixel and does not apply to phones, so a script must use phone CSS px. Standards land below the ergonomic optimum. WCAG 2.2 SC 2.5.8 (AA) requires **24 × 24 CSS px** or a 24 px spacing circle. SC 2.5.5 (AAA) asks for 44 × 44. Apple specifies 44 pt and Material 48 dp with 8 dp gaps **[S]** ([Disability World 2.5.8](https://www.disabilityworld.org/toolkit/standards/wcag/2-5-8-target-size-minimum/); [AppetiteUI](https://www.appetiteui.com/blog/touch-target-size-ios-android)). 44–48 px is about 7.3–8 mm: fine at the centre, tight at the edges. The primary bottom-of-screen action deserves about 56 px **[I]**. Large phones push the top out of one-thumb reach, and users simply re-grip ([Gizmodo](https://gizmodo.com/were-designing-apps-wrong-for-huge-smartphones-1651751412)). That makes the top corners acceptable for rare actions such as settings or close, and wrong for the primary one. One caveat applies to all of this: Hoober's grip data comes from roughly 5-inch phones in 2013. No 2024–2026 grip study of 6.1–6.9-inch phones was found.

## Attention comes from one sharpened element, not from more motion

First impressions on phones form fast and stick, as they do on desktop. Miniukovich and De Angeli confirmed "the persistence of first impressions on mobile interfaces". Computable image metrics (clutter, colour range, dominant colours, figure-ground contrast, symmetry, grid quality, white space) explained about **40% of perceived complexity and 36% of perceived aesthetics** across mobile interfaces. Clutter correlated with aesthetics at **r = −.22 at 150 ms and −.30 at 4 s** **[M]** ([Visual Impressions of Mobile App Interfaces](https://www.academia.edu/11396920/Visual_Impressions_of_Mobile_App_Interfaces); [Computation of Interface Aesthetics](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/300726008_Computation_of_Interface_Aesthetics)). The extracts did not make clear which of their 2014 and 2015 papers reports the 40%/36% figures. Nobody has re-run Tuch's desktop result on phones. That result found low complexity plus high prototypicality winning at 17–50 ms ([Google Research](https://research.google.com/pubs/archive/38315.pdf)). Extending it to phones is consistent with the evidence but remains an extrapolation.

The largest mobile dataset says what "expressive" should mean. Google's Material 3 Expressive programme ran **46 studies with 18,000+ participants**. In eye-tracking, people found key elements **up to 4× faster** in expressive designs; the example was an email Send button made bigger, differently shaped and more saturated. **Older participants found key elements as fast as younger ones**, and **87% of 18–24-year-olds** preferred the expressive versions. Google itself warned that this "is not a solution for all situations" **[M, company-reported]** ([Dezeen](https://www.dezeen.com/2025/05/28/google-ushers-in-age-of-expressive-interfaces-with-material-design-update/amp/)). The structure stayed conventional. Novelty was spent on **emphasis of the one element that matters**. On a screen the whole width of the parafovea, a sharpened focal element is seen in one glance, and adding *more* elements raises the clutter that the Miniukovich data penalises **[I]**. An attention-grabbing phone screen therefore has one dominant element made distinctive by size, shape and colour. Everything else stays typical and quiet.

The first viewport is where that element must live. NN/g's 2018 eye-tracking gave the first screenful **57% of viewing time** and the first two **74%**, down from 80% for the first screen in 2010. It was measured on desktop monitors, and no phone equivalent was found **[M, desktop]** ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/), via extract). The patterns that fail are better documented than the ones that work. Baymard found **46%** of homepage carousels implemented with usability problems. It says that on mobile they "shouldn't autorotate at all", and that static sections "perform as well with users and [are] technically much simpler" **[C]** ([Baymard](https://baymard.com/blog/homepage-carousel), via extract). Baymard's 2026 benchmark separately reports 31% done "incorrectly", a different scope. Harvard's design system **prohibits autoplaying hero videos** as disruptive to photosensitive and vestibular users ([HarvardSites](https://designsystem.harvardsites.harvard.edu/news/2025/02/autoplaying-hero-background-videos-digital-design)). iOS also forces fullscreen unless video is muted, looping and `playsinline` ([Simon Hearne](https://simonhearne.com/2021/fast-responsive-videos/)). Big top images push content below the fold and frustrate phone users ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/big-pictures-small-screens/)). The working first viewport, by consensus and inference, does four things. It says what this is in at most two lines of large type; the headline itself can be the visual. It shows **one real visual** cropped tight, never an abstract gradient field. It offers one primary action within reach. And it lets the next section **peek** into the bottom 10–15% so continuation is visible without an arrow **[I]**.

Motion grabs attention only when it is rare and caused by the user. Apple's HIG says "Don't add motion for the sake of adding motion. Gratuitous or excessive animation can distract people and may make them feel disconnected or physically uncomfortable." It asks for feedback motion that follows the gesture, for brevity, for no motion on frequent interactions, and for cancellable animations **[S]** ([Apple HIG, Motion](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/motion), fetched directly). SwiftUI made **springs the default animation**. A spring is defined by duration and bounce, and on retargeting it "carries over its current velocity", which is what makes interruption feel physical ([WWDC23 10158](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2023/10158/)). Rauno Freiberg's craft essay names the same properties: interruptibility, preserved momentum, and firing a swipe when the element arrives rather than on finger lift **[O]** ([rauno.me](https://rauno.me/craft/interaction-design)). Emil Kowalski's frequency rule is the budget. Things done 100+ times a day get no animation, occasional ones get standard motion, and rare moments may celebrate, all under about 300 ms with ease-out **[O]** ([emilkowalski/skills](https://github.com/emilkowalski/skills/blob/main/skills/review-animations/STANDARDS.md)). Imposed motion fails. NN/g found most participants "at least mildly disoriented" by scrolljacking ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolljacking-101/)). It also found that users have learned to treat parallax as visual noise, much like banner blindness ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/parallax-usability/)). Ambient motion buys no attention and costs comfort for a large population: **35.4% of US adults over 40** showed vestibular dysfunction in national survey data **[M]** ([JAMA Internal Medicine](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/773517)). Haptics follow the same logic. Apple says to "match the intensity and sharpness of a haptic with the intensity and sharpness of the animation it accompanies", and to prefer short haptics on discrete events ([Apple HIG, Playing haptics](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/playing-haptics)). On the mobile web, iOS Safari has no Vibration API, so nothing may depend on it **[I]**.

## Overload and emptiness both fail, and the phone shows both sooner

"Too much" on a phone has a few hard numbers and many heuristics. The hard ones are these. Navigation bars hold **3–5 destinations**: Android says "three to five destinations of equal importance", and Apple says avoid overflow tabs and keep labels **[S]** ([Android Developers](https://developer.android.com/develop/ui/compose/components/navigation-bar); [Apple HIG, Tab bars](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/tab-bars), both fetched directly). NN/g recommends accordion sub-navigation only for **fewer than 6 subcategories** ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mobile-subnavigation/)). Baymard measured the average checkout at **11.3 form fields in 2024** and concludes most sites need about **8** ([Baymard](https://baymard.com/blog/checkout-optimization-from-16-fields-to-8)). Everything else comes from the complexity research, which predicts appeal but publishes no thresholds. Reinecke's screenshot models, with demographics, explain **48% of appeal variance at 500 ms** ([Reinecke et al. 2013](https://dash.harvard.edu/bitstream/handle/1/12561368/Predicting%20Users%20First%20Impressions.pdf;sequence=1)). Preferred complexity varies by audience across 2.4 million ratings ([Reinecke & Gajos 2014](https://www.eecs.harvard.edu/~kgajos/papers/2014/reinecke14visual.pdf)). The per-viewport budgets in the rules section, such as one filled primary button and roughly 40–50 visible leaf elements, are therefore inferred warning levels for calibration, not findings.

Typography is where "too much" becomes physical. Lighthouse fails a page only when **40% or more of text is under 12 px** **[S]** ([Lighthouse](https://developer.chrome.com/docs/lighthouse/seo/font-size), via extract), which is far too lax for taste. iOS Safari zooms into any input whose computed font size is below **16 px**. Setting `maximum-scale=1` stops the zoom but disables pinch-zoom for everyone **[S, practitioner-documented]** ([GitLab MR](https://gitlab.com/gitlab-org/gitlab/-/merge_requests/220666)). Butterick's range is 45–90 characters per line ([Practical Typography](https://practicaltypography.com/line-length.html)), and secondary mobile bands of 35–50 or 40–60 conflict ([UXPin](https://www.uxpin.com/studio/blog/optimal-line-length-for-readability/)). With 16 px side gutters, the 390 px column is 358 px. That gives about **41–47 characters at 16 px and 36–41 at 18 px** **[D]**, so phone body text sits at the bottom of the classical range by necessity. Headlines break first. A 72 px desktop display line fits only about **5–7 characters** across 358 px, so "Infrastructure" overflows. The phone H1 belongs at about 32–44 px, roughly 2–2.75× body **[D/I]**. The repo's probe currently warns when the largest type is under 2.5× body. That threshold fits 1440 but would push phones toward overflow; at 390 the useful range is about 2–3×.

Hidden-content patterns make "too much" worse rather than better. Baymard's participants repeatedly **overlooked core product content behind horizontal tabs**, and vertically collapsed sections did not show the problem ([Baymard](https://baymard.com/blog/avoid-horizontal-tabs)). NN/g finds carousels on touch screens have sequential access and low discoverability, and their dot indicators make poor touch controls ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mobile-carousels/)). Horizontal rows need an explicit "+3" truncation tile or clear arrows; a half-visible item is a weaker cue ([Baymard](https://baymard.com/blog/truncating-product-gallery-thumbnails)). NN/g recommends accordions on mobile because headers form a "mini-IA". It claims reading on mobile is about twice as difficult as on desktop ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mobile-accordions/)). Another NN/g study, however, found "no practical differences" in comprehension between phone and computer, only slower reading ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mobile-content/)). The newer finding fits better: phones do not need dumbed-down content, they need **structured** content. The desktop-to-phone translation is "same content, different packaging" **[I]**. Grids become one column (two for image or product grids). Sidebars become inline sections or drawers. Wide tables get a locked header and a column choice or stacked rows ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/data-tables/)). Hover-only actions become visible controls. Decision-critical content is never deleted.

"Too little" fails more often in AI output and gets less attention. NN/g's **illusion of completeness** (Tognazzini's term from 1998) describes a hero or whitespace band that fills the screen so the page looks finished and nobody scrolls; the remedy is visible peeking content ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/illusion-of-completeness/)). A `100vh` centred headline over a gradient spends the most-viewed screen on zero information, and on a 390 × 844 phone browser chrome makes the visible area smaller still **[I]**. This repo's blind test found the same at desktop scale. The owner preferred a richer, conventional portfolio with a sticky nav, meta strip, clock, focus strip and dark contact block over a sparser, structurally novel one that passed every lint check **[R, n = 1]** ([experiments/results.md](../experiments/results.md)). Combined with Reinecke's finding that preference is not maximised at minimum complexity, the target is **low-to-moderate, well-grouped complexity**. A screen should be rich in parts that are grouped by alignment and space, not boxed and multiplied **[I]**. Desktop section padding copied straight to the phone (more than about 96–120 px top and bottom at 390) is a common thin-page smell. Mobile rhythm usually sits around 48–80 px, though that is practitioner convention with no source in these notes.

## Uniqueness lives in the surface while the controls stay standard

The safe and unsafe zones for novelty on phones can be named, and the evidence is strongest at the structural end. NN/g's 179-person study found **hidden navigation less discoverable and slower** than visible or combo navigation. The penalty was smaller on mobile than on desktop, but it was real **[M]** ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/find-navigation-mobile-even-hamburger/), via extract). Secondary figures such as "15% slower on mobile" are unverified. Facebook's iOS app reported better engagement, satisfaction and perceived speed after moving from a hamburger to a bottom tab bar, and Polar and Zeebox lost engagement moving the other way. These are company-reported and about a decade old ([LukeW](https://www.lukew.com/ff/entry.asp?1945=)). Apple's current tab-bar guidance, fetched directly, is unambiguous: "Make sure the tab bar is visible… If you hide the tab bar, people can forget which area of the app they're in." It also says "Don't disable or hide tab bar buttons" and "Use a tab bar to support navigation, not to provide actions" **[S]** ([Apple HIG, Tab bars](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/tab-bars)). Gestures are the least discoverable layer. Apple says to avoid "using a familiar gesture… to perform an action that's unique to your app", and to "use shortcut gestures to supplement standard gestures, not replace them", keeping Back even with edge-swipe ([Apple HIG, Gestures](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/gestures)). NN/g adds that swipes need a visible cue, that one-time onboarding tips are forgotten, and that one swipe should not mean different things in different parts of a screen ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/contextual-swipe/)).

Apple's own Liquid Glass shows the cost of novelty in the control layer. NN/g's heuristic review of iOS 26 said translucency "obscures content instead of letting it take the spotlight". It found cramped tab bars with shrunken targets, called the motion "distraction with a side of nausea", and said relocating search broke learned habits. Apple shipped a "Tinted" option in iOS 26.1 betas after the backlash **[C, heuristic review]** ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/liquid-glass/); [Gulf News](https://gulfnews.com/technology/companies/apple-yields-tinted-control-in-ios-261-beta-4-tones-down-liquid-glass-after-backlash-1.500315176)). The acclaimed examples put their signature elsewhere. Airbnb's 2025 redesign kept its tab bar and search-first structure and made **animated 3D icons** the signature, with a house whose "teeny front door pops open" ([Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-06-13/apple-airbnb-ditch-flat-app-icons-for-new-3d-ui-design)). Duolingo keeps a conventional lesson flow and makes its characters react to state through Rive state machines **[O, vendor]** ([Rive](https://rive.app/blog/duolingo-s-ai-powered-video-call-brings-lily-to-life)). Family's wallet is remembered for sheets that expand and adapt, and for small rewards such as confetti on backup completion ([Family](https://family.co/blog/launch)). Even award juries gate on usability. An Awwwards Mobile Excellence badge requires **75/100 or more** on Google-derived checks for viewport, tap targets, font legibility and contrast. Mobile Site of the Year weighs creativity as one of four criteria **[C]** ([Awwwards Mobile Excellence guidelines](https://www.awwwards.com/mobile-excellence-guidelines.pdf)). One 2026 blog says 70, which conflicts with the official figure.

| Zone | What goes here | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Never break** | Visible primary navigation (3–5 labelled tabs in apps; visible top-level links or a bottom bar on the web); Back plus system edge-swipe; native-speed vertical scroll; tap = activate; 44–48 px targets; legible, high-contrast text and controls; one sheet at a time | NN/g hidden-nav study [M]; Apple and Android guidance [S]; Liquid Glass review [C] |
| **Bend with care** | Sheets with custom detents; swipe actions that *also* exist as buttons; horizontal rails with a visibly peeking next item; bars that minimise on scroll | Apple Sheets and Gestures [S]; Baymard truncation [C] |
| **Free to own** | Display typography; colour; one illustration, icon or photo language; image crops; the motion personality of transitions; haptic accents; empty and success states; copy voice | Airbnb, Duolingo, Family [O]; M3 Expressive [M, company] |

This is the MAYA principle (most advanced, yet acceptable) mapped onto a phone, and it matches the repo's blind-test lesson: **novelty in structure lost, and the typical structure with a rich surface won** **[R/I]**. Generic AI output fails the "free to own" column, not the "never break" one. On a phone, the practitioner-catalogued tells become one long column of identical rounded cards (icon-in-square, heading, two grey lines). They also include a centred pill badge over a centred headline and a purple gradient orb, uniform 16–24 px radii with soft shadows, a thin coloured left border, fade-up on every block, and tiny grey 12–13 px secondary text **[O]** ([925 Studios](https://www.925studios.co/blog/ai-slop-design-tells); [DEV](https://dev.to/james_anderson_h/the-purple-gradient-problem-why-ai-ui-all-looks-alike-and-how-to-fix-it-3j65)). The replacements below are grounded in the evidence above. Instead of a card stack, use unboxed rows on one left edge plus one featured item at a larger size; Apple notes that "people assume that aligned items are related" ([Apple HIG, Layout](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/layout)). Use left-aligned text, centring only single lines. Make the headline the visual, set against one real image. Separate secondary text by weight or colour at 4.5:1, never by shrinking it. Use a radius *system* rather than a default radius, and add elevation only to things that float. The tells list stays calibration, not law. The owner's own taste included several "tells", and a liked design that uses them well should be fixed for correctness, not purged.

## Comfortable eyes need size, polarity and stillness, not blue-light fixes

Digital eye strain is common, at an estimated **50% or more** of computer users. It has two families of causes: focusing and binocular load, and dry eye from reduced blinking **[M]** ([Sheppard & Wolffsohn 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29963645/), via extract). The AAO, as reported in the press, puts the blink drop at about **15 to 7 per minute** on screens and recommends 20-20-20 breaks ([HealthDay citing AAO](https://www.healthday.com/health-news/eye-care/screens-are-everywhere-resolve-to-protect-your-vision)). A UI cannot change blink rate, session length or OLED PWM flicker. Whether PWM hurts sensitive users is unestablished, and the only cited paper has a conflict of interest ([ElcomSoft](https://blog.elcomsoft.com/2018/03/iphone-x-eye-strain-how-to-stop-oled-flickering-in-just-three-clicks/)). What a UI *can* change is text size, polarity, contrast, luminance, saturation, flashing and motion, and whether it honours user settings. "Eye-saver" claims beyond that are marketing.

Size is the first lever, and vision science gives a floor. Reading speed is maximal for x-heights subtending about **0.2° to 2°**, and falls below the critical print size of about 0.2° **[M]** ([Legge & Bigelow 2011](https://legge.dl8.umn.edu/sites/legge.psych.umn.edu/files/2020-08/Legge%20Bigelow%202011%20Does%20size%20matter%20for%20reading%3F%20A%20review%20of%20findings%20from%20vision%20science%20and%20typography.pdf)). At 32 cm, with a sans x-height of about 0.52 em, the arithmetic below follows **[D]**. Bababekova's measured 0.8 M sits at about 0.21°, so people already read phone pages at the critical size, with no margin to go smaller.

| CSS size | x-height | Angle at 32 cm | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| 11 px | ~0.94 mm | ~0.17° | Below critical size: non-essential labels only |
| 12 px | ~1.03 mm | ~0.18° | Captions and metadata only |
| 14 px | ~1.20 mm | ~0.21° | At the critical size: short secondary text |
| 16 px | ~1.37 mm | ~0.25° | **Body floor** |
| 17–18 px | ~1.5 mm | ~0.27° | Comfortable long reading |

Apple's smallest text styles are 11 pt and its body at the xSmall setting is 14 pt. The 17 pt default body is standard HIG knowledge but was not re-confirmed this session ([Apple HIG, Typography](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/typography)). Font choice matters most at small sizes. Below the critical size, Times was read up to 50% slower than Courier ([Mansfield, Legge & Bane 1996](https://legge.dl8.umn.edu/sites/legge.psych.umn.edu/files/files/media/mansfield96_psychophysics_of_reading_xv-_font_effects_in_normal_and_low_vision.pdf)), and a generous x-height helps. Type must be set in `rem` so that Dynamic Type and Android font scaling work, and layouts must survive WCAG 1.4.12's spacing overrides: 1.5× line height, 0.12 em letter spacing and 0.16 em word spacing **[S]** ([WCAG 2.2](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/#text-spacing)).

Polarity is the second lever, and the evidence runs against the popular belief. For normal vision, **dark text on a light background wins at every age** ([Piepenbrock et al. 2013](https://documentacion.fundacionmapfre.org/documentacion/publico/en/bib/150136.do)). The advantage **grows linearly as characters shrink** ([Piepenbrock et al. 2014](https://metatoc.com/papers/26930-positive-display-polarity-is-particularly-advantageous-for-small-character-sizes-implications-for-display-design)). **Light-on-dark in a dark room is the least legible condition**, which is exactly when people switch dark mode on. The proposed mechanism is that a brighter display constricts the pupil and reduces optical aberration **[M]** ([Dobres et al. 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28166901/)). Dark mode remains legitimate as a preference, for lower total light at night, for some low-vision users, and for OLED battery. It just isn't "healthier". Done well, dark means a **#121212-class base rather than #000** for reading surfaces, about 15.8:1 against white. It also means off-white or about 87% white text, and lighter, desaturated accents, because saturated colours "visually vibrate" on dark grounds **[S]** ([Material Design 2, Dark theme](https://m2.material.io/design/color/dark-theme)). "Pure black on white is tiring" has no controlled support. Softening to #111 on #FAFAFA is a harmless aesthetic choice that keeps about 17–19:1, not a health requirement. Contrast also needs headroom for daylight. Ambient reflection adds similar luminance to text and background and compresses the effective ratio, so body text should aim at about **7:1** rather than scraping 4.5:1 **[D]**. APCA catches thin or small light-on-dark text that passes WCAG 2. It sets **Lc 75** as the body minimum at 18 px/400 and **Lc 90** as preferred at 14 px/400, but it is not normative ([Myndex apca-w3](https://github.com/Myndex/apca-w3)). The sensible split is to **gate on WCAG 2 AA and warn on APCA**.

Colour and motion are the third lever. Saturated red against blue (and, less strongly, red against green) produces chromostereopsis, a depth shift that is "hard and tiring to look at or read" **[C]** ([Weinschenk](https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/100-things-every/9780136746959/ch10.xhtml)). One small study reported saturated blue text on dark as the hardest to focus on, but its primary source could not be confirmed **[weak]**. Saturated colour therefore belongs on small areas such as buttons, badges and charts, never on body text or behind reading text. Blue light is a myth as a design concern. Cochrane's review of **17 RCTs** found probably **no short-term benefit** of blue-filtering lenses for visual fatigue **[M]** ([City St George's](https://www.citystgeorges.ac.uk/news-and-events/news/2023/08/blue-light-filtering-spectacles-probably-no-difference-eye-strain-visual-performance-sleep-quality)), and the AAO links strain to overuse, not blue light. A taste skill should never produce sepia "eye-saver" palettes as a health feature. Motion has hard rules. WCAG 2.3.1 forbids flashing more than **three times per second** above threshold, and the area threshold is defined within a 10° visual field. At phone distance a 10° field is about **56 mm, essentially the full screen width** **[S/D]** ([WCAG 2.2](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/#three-flashes-or-below-threshold)). Any full-screen motion on a phone is therefore "large" in the visual field, which is why parallax, scroll-linked transforms and big slides belong behind `prefers-reduced-motion: no-preference` ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/CSS/@media/prefers-reduced-motion)). WCAG 2.2.2 requires a pause control for any auto-motion lasting more than 5 seconds. The page cannot know the room it is read in, but the OS does. Honouring `prefers-color-scheme`, `prefers-contrast`, `prefers-reduced-motion`, `forced-colors` and OS text scaling is the only reliable environmental adaptation **[S]** ([MDN media features](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/CSS/@media#media_features)).

## Rules for the taste skill, split between the 390 px probe and judgement

### How much to trust the numbers

Confidence differs by source family, and the session's network limits drove much of that difference. **High**: WCAG thresholds (stable normative text, even though w3.org was read via extracts), and Apple and Android guidance (fetched directly). **Medium**: NN/g and Baymard findings and percentages, Google's indexing statements and Lighthouse thresholds. These were all read through search extracts; the direction is reliable, but exact figures need a check against the page before public quotation. **Medium-low**: traffic splits by industry (secondary aggregators, mutually conflicting), Hoober's grip and location sizes (dated, conflicting edge values, read via extract), and Material 3 Expressive's 4× (company-reported). **Low**: every per-viewport budget marked **I** below. These are reasoned starting points that this repo's blind tests should calibrate, and a measured reference or the owner's stated taste may override any WARN. ## Conclusion

The question "should mobile be the priority?" turns out to be malformed for a taste skill. The phone is not a smaller desktop that deserves first or second place. It is a different viewing situation: a 12° window at 32 cm, gripped in shifting ways, seen whole in one glance, and used for the first impression rather than the deep read. That changes what the skill should optimise. On a phone, attention is won by **subtraction plus one sharpened element**, not by addition. Comfort comes from size, polarity and stillness rather than softened palettes. Distinctiveness has to live in the surface, because the control layer is where even Apple paid for novelty. The skill's real risk is not neglecting mobile. It is producing a centred phone column everywhere and calling that "mobile-first".

Two implications go beyond the individual rules. First, the probe's thresholds should become **width-aware**. Several of today's desktop-calibrated rules, such as the 2.5× headline ratio and the 13 px body floor, are wrong at 390, and the most valuable new checks (parity, false floor, input size, 200% text, hover-only content) exist only at phone width. Second, the evidence for "too little" is as strong as the evidence for "too much", but only the "too much" side has standards behind it. A skill driven mainly by lint will therefore drift toward austerity unless critique explicitly checks for richness. The inferred budgets here are the place to spend the next blind tests: run the same content at 390 with high and low part counts and peek versus full-height heroes, and let the owner's choices set the numbers the literature could not.
