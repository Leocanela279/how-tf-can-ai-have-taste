# Diagnose first, then change the cheapest layer

Redesigning an existing UI means choosing **which layer** to change. The layers run from a targeted fix, through a token-level restyle and a layout realignment, to a structural redesign of navigation and flows, and finally to a rebrand. The main thing that sets the risk is how much users have to relearn, not how different the result looks. A motive is valid when it points to a symptom you can show: a contrast failure, a task people fail, a funnel step that leaks, a Core Web Vital over its threshold, measurable inconsistency, or a first impression that reads as generic. "We're bored", "a competitor redesigned" and "it's been three years" are not valid motives. The measured evidence on first impressions favours pages that are **simple and typical of their category**. So the common requests ("it looks AI-made", "it looks dated", "it's too busy") almost always call for a surface change to tokens, hierarchy, type calibration and copy, and almost never for new navigation. When the palette, fonts and logo are fixed, the levers that remain are **relationships and quantities**: lightness steps and contrast, how much of the screen the brand colour covers, the ink staircase, how many sizes and weights are used, spacing rhythm, how many icons there are and how consistent they are, how strong borders are, and which element leads. Linear, Stripe and GitHub all kept their identity and regenerated exactly these. Every famous failure (Digg v4, Snapchat 2018, Skype 2017, Sonos 2024) was a forced, all-at-once switch that removed or moved something users relied on. The rollouts that worked shipped in reversible stages with an opt-out. The AI skills that handle redesign well (Impeccable, taste-skill, Emil Kowalski's audit skills, Superdesign) all follow the same sequence: classify the request, record the before state, diagnose, stop and let the user choose, change things in a fixed low-risk order, then prove the result against the before. The report ends with a ten-stage procedure for a `taste-redesign` skill. That skill reuses taste's probe, shoot, lint, library and critic, and adds four new things: classifying the request into a mode, a must-survive parity inventory, a token-drift inventory, and critique against the *before* page. Confidence is moderate. Most rules are practitioner consensus or synthesis, not measurements. Several primary sources (NN/g, Google Research, Linear, Stripe, GitHub, Vercel and Emil's essays) could be read only through search summaries in this session.

## Ten questions, short answers

How to read the evidence. Each rule carries one of three tags. **[measured]** means a controlled study, a large dataset, or published figures (earnings, traffic panels, opt-out rates) produced the number. **[consensus]** means several recognised practitioners, design-system teams or skill authors say the same thing independently, but nothing was measured. **[synthesis]** is this report's own reasoning from the evidence, and is untested. A **†** after a citation means the source domain was blocked in this session (nngroup.com, research.google, library.gv.com, archive.uie.com, linear.app, stripe.com, github.blog, vercel.com, emilkowal.ski, rauno.me, dev.to and others), so the claim rests on search-result summaries or indexed abstracts. Those claims agree with each other across several summaries, but their exact wording is unverified. Case accounts (what Linear or Snapchat did) are cited facts, not rules, and are not tagged.

## A redesign is a choice of layer, and relearning cost sets the risk

There is no standard vocabulary. Agencies use "refresh" and "redesign" interchangeably ([WebFX](https://www.webfx.com/blog/web-design/website-refresh-vs-redesign/); [Oyova](https://www.oyova.com/blog/gradual-changes-at-your-website-vs-relaunch/)). What does hold up across sources is the distinction by layer. A refresh changes the visual skin and keeps information architecture, navigation, flows and URLs. A redesign "often reconsiders" structure, and a refresh "more easily maintains current SEO efforts" ([Webstacks](https://www.webstacks.com/blog/website-refresh-vs-website-redesign); [Huemor](https://huemor.rocks/blog/website-refresh-vs-redesign/)). A rebrand changes identity (name, logo, voice), and a visual refresh usually follows it. Iterative improvement, a stream of small measured changes, is what the usability establishment recommends as the default. UX Myths lists "you need to redesign your website periodically" as a myth ([UX Myths](https://uxmyths.com/post/712537920/myth-you-need-to-redesign-your-website-periodically)). NN/g's Loranger says to "never make radical changes when minimal adjustments will suffice" ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/radical-incremental-redesign/)†).

For an agent this collapses into a working ladder of five modes, ordered by disruption **[synthesis]**. A **targeted fix** repairs one defect, such as a contrast failure or a broken focus state. A **polish** keeps the look and fixes drift, defects and missing states. A **restyle** keeps layout and IA and changes tokens: type calibration, colour, spacing, radii, icons and imagery. A **realign** changes hierarchy, grouping, density and layouts inside the same pages and flows. A **redesign** changes navigation, templates, flows or the content model. A rebrand sits beside the ladder because it changes identity and usually forces a restyle. The line that matters for risk is between "surface", meaning anything a user does not have to relearn, and "structure", meaning anything that moves where things are or changes how a task is done. The backlash literature is consistent on this point. Spool's critique of Google's change-aversion framing is that users don't hate change; they hate losing accumulated expertise ([UIE](https://archive.uie.com/brainsparks/2012/07/16/googles-take-on-change-aversion-misses-the-point/)†). In the case studies, the navigation and IA redesigns at Vercel and Stripe drew louder pushback than the pure tone-and-spacing recalibrations at Linear, which matches that reading ([Vercel Community](https://community.vercel.com/t/layout-change-of-the-dashboard-it-sucks/35367)).

The AI-skill authors reached the same conclusion from watching agents fail. The taste-skill classic version states that "misclassifying the mode is the single biggest source of bad redesign output." It splits redesign into *preserve* ("audit first, extract brand tokens, evolve gradually") and *overhaul* ("treat as greenfield for visuals; preserve content and IA") ([taste-skill-classics §11](https://github.com/Leonxlnx/taste-skill-classics/blob/main/skills/taste-skill-v2-experimental/SKILL.md)). Impeccable puts the rule more sharply: "**Refinement preserves; redesign replaces** … Never split the difference into polish on the discarded look" ([Impeccable SKILL.md](https://github.com/pbakaus/impeccable/blob/main/.devin/skills/impeccable/SKILL.md)). The failure it names is the half-redesign: new spacing on the old palette and type, which is neither the incumbent world tightened nor a new one. That is the outcome an unclassified "make it better" request tends to produce. The second half of Impeccable's rule matters just as much: "Polish is refinement, never concealed redesign … If the concept itself is wrong, say so and recommend redesign … instead of smuggling in a replacement" ([polish.md](https://github.com/pbakaus/impeccable/blob/main/.devin/skills/impeccable/reference/polish.md)). **Classify first and never mix modes** is the strongest consensus rule in this report **[consensus]**.

## Seven motives survive scrutiny when tied to a symptom; boredom and calendars do not

### What each motive actually licenses

The useful test of a motive is whether it points at a symptom you can show, and the symptom then tells you which layer to touch **[synthesis]**. The table assigns each motive the owner raised to the layer the evidence supports.

| Motive | Valid when | Layer it usually licenses | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Accessibility** | Contrast, labels, alt text, focus or keyboard failures are found | Fix or restyle (colour tokens, focus styles, semantics) | [measured]: WebAIM Million 2026 reports detectable WCAG failures on about 95.9% of the top million home pages, and low-contrast text on **83.9%**, the most common failure for the eighth year ([WebAIM](https://webaim.org/projects/million/); 2026 figures via [Priority Pixels](https://prioritypixels.co.uk/insights/web-accessibility-is-getting-worse-according-to-the-2026-webaim-million-report/)). The European Accessibility Act has applied since 28 June 2025. Check the DOJ Title II dates before quoting them ([digitalapplied](https://www.digitalapplied.com/blog/web-accessibility-statistics-2026-wcag-lawsuit-data)) |
| **Performance** | LCP > 2.5 s, INP > 200 ms or CLS > 0.1 at p75 | Usually code, not design. Design is responsible only when the design causes the cost (hero video, large blur, layout that shifts) | Thresholds [consensus] ([web.dev](https://web.dev/articles/vitals)) |
| **"Not intuitive"** | Users fail tasks or click wrong first; support tickets cluster | Labels first, then flow or IA (structure) | [consensus]; requires behavioural evidence |
| **Conversion** | A specific funnel step leaks | That step's form, copy and error handling (local structure) | [consensus]; Baymard puts average cart abandonment near 70%, but the figure updates yearly and was not re-verified ([Baymard](https://baymard.com/lists/cart-abandonment-rate)) |
| **Generic, AI-made or dated look** | First impression reads as unowned defaults to the target audience | Restyle: type, palette usage, imagery, copy, icon consistency. **Not** new navigation | [measured] for first-impression effects; the slop diagnosis is [consensus] |
| **"Too much"** | Many elements compete; no single focal point in a blur test | Realign: hierarchy, grouping, fewer competing colours | [measured]: visual complexity is the strongest predictor of appeal |
| **"Not enough"** | Low information scent; screens read as thin | Realign: more real content and parts, a tighter spacing scale | [measured here]: blind test B4 |
| **Brand** | Positioning or identity actually changed | Rebrand, then restyle; structure only if scope changed | [consensus] |
| **Tech or design-system debt** | Measurable token drift (dozens of greys, 11 font sizes), hard-coded values, forked components | A token refactor that is **visually neutral first**, then a restyle through the tokens | [consensus] |

Two motives need extra care. **"It looks AI-made" is valid but narrow.** Users form aesthetic judgements within **17–50 ms**, and sites with **low visual complexity and high prototypicality** (simple and familiar) are rated most appealing. Unfamiliar sites were rated less attractive even when they were simple ([Tuch et al. 2012, IJHCS](https://research.google/pubs/the-role-of-visual-complexity-and-prototypicality-regarding-first-impression-of-websites-working-towards-understanding-aesthetic-judgments/)†; [Google Research blog](https://research.google/blog/users-love-simple-and-familiar-designs-why-websites-need-to-make-a-great-first-impression/)†). Reinecke et al. modelled about **half the variance** in 500 ms appeal ratings from image statistics across 450 sites. Complexity mattered far more than colourfulness, and first impressions were "influential enough to later affect their opinions of a site's usability and trustworthiness" ([Reinecke et al. 2013](https://kgajos.seas.harvard.edu/papers/reinecke13aesthetics.pdf)) **[measured]**. The slop look therefore justifies change, but the evidence says to fix it by **lowering complexity and owning the surface while keeping the structure typical**, not by inventing a novel layout. Practitioners add that the problem is *unchosen defaults*, not any single colour. A brand that owns purple can keep purple, and banning one tell just moves the model to the next most probable look ([prg.sh](https://prg.sh/ramblings/Why-Your-AI-Keeps-Building-the-Same-Purple-Gradient-Website); [Kosta Canatselis](https://world.hey.com/kostac/spot-the-slop-a-ui-designer-s-guide-to-fixing-ai-defaults-4c448c9c)) **[consensus]**. No peer-reviewed study yet measures how users perceive "AI-looking" UI specifically.

**"Too much" and "not enough" are both real, and they pull against each other.** The complexity finding says busy pages lose. This repo's blind test B4 found the opposite failure. The owner chose a rich no-skill portfolio over the skill's sparser build, even though the gate scored it worse, and taste-skill's own blind tests report that "a page that has lost its labels, rules and cards and gained nothing in their place reads as thin" ([experiments/results.md](../experiments/results.md); [taste-skill](https://github.com/Leonxlnx/taste-skill/blob/main/skills/taste-skill/SKILL.md)). The two fit together once "complexity" is read as *perceived* complexity: the fix for "too much" is **grouping**, not deleting. Dense work tools are a separate case with no comparable data. There, density serves expert users, and stripping it out can hurt task success **[synthesis]**.

### What the invalid motives have in common

The weak motives (boredom, a calendar, copying a competitor's format) share one property: none of them names anything the user is failing at. The failure cases show what happens when the company's agenda replaces the user's job. Digg v4 served publishers through RSS auto-submission and removed the bury button. Snapchat and Skype grafted on Stories-style formats. Instagram chased TikTok parity. All four ended in partial or full reversal ([TechCrunch on Digg](https://techcrunch.com/2011/02/11/digg-removing-rss-option-for-publishers); [TechCrunch on Skype](https://techcrunch.com/2018/09/03/skype-rolls-back-its-redesign-by-ditching-stories-squiggles-and-over-the-top-color); [CNN on Instagram](https://us.cnn.com/2022/07/28/tech/instagram-walks-back-updates)). Christina Wodtke's summary is "Users don't hate change, they hate you" ([eleganthack](https://eleganthack.com/user-dont-hate-change-they-hate-you)). Laura Klein adds the uncomfortable corollary: "Sometimes the new design is simply worse" ([O'Reilly, *Managing Startups* ch. 31](https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/managing-startups-best/9781449370473/ch31.html)) **[consensus]**. The often-quoted claims that "80% of redesigns fail" and that redesigns cost "10–40% of organic traffic" are vendor marketing with no methodology, and this report does not rely on them ([Lollypop](https://lollypop.design/blog/2025/september/why-80-percent-website-redesigns-fail/)).

## One evaluator finds a third of the problems, so measure what a script can

### Four kinds of diagnosis, and what a solo developer or an agent can run

Diagnosis methods fall into four families, and each answers a different question. **Inspection** (heuristic evaluation, consistency and accessibility audits) is cheap and finds *likely* problems. **Behavioural data** (analytics, funnels, HEART metrics) says *where* and *how much*. **Observation** (usability testing) says *why*. **Perception tests** (5-second, first-click, preference) measure first impressions and findability ([Design Monks](https://www.designmonks.co/blog/ux-audit-vs-heuristic-evaluation); [HEART, Rodden et al. 2010](https://research.google.com/pubs/archive/36299.pdf)†) **[consensus]**. The best-measured number in this area should temper every AI audit. In Nielsen and Molich's original study, single evaluators "found between 20 and 51% of the usability problems", and three to five evaluators together performed well ([Nielsen & Molich 1990](https://blogs.fu-berlin.de/hci2023/files/2023/06/Nielsen-and-Molich-1990-Heuristic-evaluation-of-user-interfaces.pdf)). Later aggregations put five evaluators at about 75%, varying from 55% to 90% by interface ([MeasuringU](https://measuringu.com/he-multiple/)) **[measured]**. An agent's audit is one evaluator. It should say so, and it should treat structural conclusions as hypotheses **[synthesis]**.

That leads to a minimum viable diagnosis that splits cleanly between what an agent can do alone and what needs the user **[synthesis]**. The agent can run, unaided: automated accessibility and Core Web Vitals checks, a token inventory, a blur test for the focal point, a 5-second read of a screenshot ("what is this, who is it for, what's the next action?"), a count of elements competing above the fold, a generic-defaults check, and a heuristic walk over the primary tasks. The user has to supply the goal, the 1–3 primary tasks, and any real analytics, support complaints or user quotes. Without behavioural data, IA and flow changes are guesses, and the agent should propose them rather than ship them. HEART's discipline of **Goals → Signals → Metrics**, where every metric traces to a stated goal, is the right frame for writing those proposals ([Amplitude explainer](https://www.amplitude.com/blog/heart-framework-software-ux.md)).

### The token inventory answers "how do you know what to change"

The most mechanical diagnostic is also the most revealing for a visual refresh. Brad Frost calls the interface inventory, an extraction of every colour, type style, button and spacing value actually in use, "a critical first step" ([Brad Frost](https://bradfrost.com/blog/post/conducting-an-interface-inventory/)). Real audits show what drift looks like. One found **85 unique colours**, including what "looks like 50 shades of grey" ([OpenClassrooms](https://openclassrooms.com/en/courses/4556206-design-the-visual-side-of-experiences-ui-design/5259131-explore-the-benefits-of-design-systems)). Another found **7 text styles, 11 font sizes and 4 button styles** on one homepage ([RadPad inventory](https://medium.com/@itsjustmath/radpad-interface-inventory-6164f62c9cc4)). Single cases like these are illustrations, not benchmarks, and no source gives a "normal" count. **The values with the most variants are where calibration has drifted**, so the inventory output is itself a ranked list of what to change **[synthesis]**. GitHub's Primer team shows the method at scale. They "immediately identified over 100 color pairs" of text on background, then replaced manual checks with a script that runs contrast checks for all tokens across all themes ([GitHub Blog](https://github.blog/engineering/user-experience/unlocking-inclusive-design-how-primers-color-system-is-making-github-com-more-inclusive/)†).

### Mapping what you see to the lever that fixes it

The owner asked whether the problem is icons, colours, font, title hierarchy, layout, spacing or copy. The answer is to read the symptom, measure it, and pick the lever with the lowest relearning cost that fixes it. The ordering below follows the one measured comparison available, in which complexity outweighed colourfulness ([Reinecke et al. 2013](https://kgajos.seas.harvard.edu/papers/reinecke13aesthetics.pdf)), plus the case evidence that follows. No study ranks individual visual levers against each other, so the rows below are **[synthesis]** unless marked.

| What you see | What to measure | Lever | Layer, relearning cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nothing leads; the eye wanders | Blur test: how many focal candidates? Count competing elements above the fold | **Hierarchy**: one lead element per view; de-emphasise competitors (smaller, lighter, lower-contrast labels) rather than making the lead louder | Surface or realign, low |
| Headings don't step; titles look the same as body | Distinct sizes and weights in use; display-to-body ratio | **Type scale within the family**: 3–5 sizes with one decisive jump, at most 3 weights, tighter tracking and leading on large type | Surface, none |
| Looks off but you can't say why | Count of unique colours, sizes, spacings and radii; near-duplicates | **Consolidate tokens**; spacing between groups at least 2× spacing inside them; one radius rule (nested radius = inner + padding) | Surface, none |
| Busy, colourful, noisy | Share of screen in saturated colour; colour on decoration | **Colour usage**: brand colour on action and active state only; neutral chrome; colour removed from decorative tiles | Surface, none |
| Washed out or hard to read | Contrast per text node; ink staircase | **Ink staircase**: 3–4 inks with known ratios; body text at 4.5:1 or above | Surface, none ([measured] prevalence) |
| Icon soup | Icon count, families, stroke widths, sizes | **Icons**: fewer, smaller, one family and one stroke; drop icon tiles above headings | Surface, none |
| Generic or AI-made | Tells present without a reason ([tells.md](../skills/taste/references/tells.md)); copy specificity | **Type calibration, owned imagery, specific copy**, defaults removed *and replaced* | Surface, none |
| Too sparse | Words and parts per screen; empty bands | **Density**: real content, meta, figures; a tighter spacing scale | Realign, low |
| Wrong first clicks; can't find things | First-click on top tasks; support tickets | **Navigation labels first**, then IA | Structure, high |
| Drop-off at one step | Funnel step rate | That step's form, copy and errors | Local structure, medium |
| Slow or janky | LCP, INP, CLS; bytes; blur | Images, fonts, scripts, reserved space | Code, none |

The rule underneath the table is to **touch cheap-to-learn layers first** (tokens, hierarchy, copy). Touch expensive ones (navigation, IA, flows) only when there is evidence of findability or task failure, or when the user explicitly asks and accepts the relearning cost **[synthesis from consensus]**.

## With palette, fonts and logo fixed, redesign the relationships between them

### The flagship refreshes kept identity and regenerated the relationships

None of the product redesigns studied changed its logo, and most kept the typeface family. What they changed was **how the existing values relate to each other**. These accounts were read through search summaries only.

Linear's March 2024 redesign rebuilt themes so each is generated from **three inputs (base colour, accent colour, contrast) instead of 98 hand-defined variables**. It works in LCH so that equal lightness values look equally bright, and a single contrast input scales text and border intensity together ([Linear](https://linear.app/now/how-we-redesigned-the-linear-ui)†). Its stated aim was "to improve hierarchy, balance, and density" ([Linear changelog](https://linear.app/changelog/2024-03-20-new-linear-ui)†).

Linear's March 2026 refresh changed no brand asset at all, only **how attention is allocated**. Sidebars went "a few shades dimmer" so content leads. Desktop tabs became more compact. There are **fewer icons, at smaller sizes**. **Coloured team-icon backgrounds were removed**. Borders and separators were softened. The governing principle was "**Don't compete for attention you haven't earned**." The system was also recalibrated in place while other teams kept shipping on top of it ([Linear](https://linear.app/now/behind-the-latest-design-refresh)†).

Stripe kept its hues, moved its scales into CIELAB, and adjusted them by perceptual lightness. The result was a rule built into the system: any two colours **five levels apart pass small-text contrast**, and four levels apart pass for icons and large text ([Stripe](https://stripe.com/blog/accessible-color-systems)†).

GitHub regenerated its Primer primitives so that a fix at the base layer carries up through functional and component tokens. Its two inverted neutral scales were tested against minimum contrast ([primer/primitives v9.0.0](https://github.com/primer/primitives/releases/tag/v9.0.0)).

Google reports that Material 3 Expressive, built on the same foundations but with bolder emphasis through colour, shape, size contrast and motion, helped participants spot key elements "up to 4× faster" across **46 studies with more than 18,000 participants** ([Material blog](https://m3.material.io/blog/building-with-m3-expressive)). The 4× figure is a best case from one eye-tracking test, not an average ([Android Police](https://www.androidpolice.com/im-excited-about-material-3-expressive/)), and nobody has replicated it independently.

The common move across Linear, Stripe and GitHub was **keep the hues, regenerate lightness and contrast in a perceptual space, then lock the relationship into a rule**. That is the "taste is calibration" belief of the existing taste skill, applied to a redesign.

### Why the fixed assets should stay fixed, and the levers that remain

Brand science explains why keeping the constants is right and not merely conservative. Jenni Romaniuk's Distinctive Asset Grid scores each asset on **fame** (people link it to the brand) and **uniqueness** (they link it to no other). An asset above both thresholds can stand alone for the brand and should be protected ([The Brand Algorithm](https://www.the-brand-algorithm.com/distinctive-asset-grid/); summaries of Romaniuk 2018, primary text not read). A 2026 peer-reviewed benchmark found the average asset scores only **26% fame and 54% uniqueness**, and that shape-based assets are strongest ([Int. J. of Advertising 2026](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/02650487.2026.2637295)) **[measured]**. Most assets are weak, so the few that do reach recognition (typically the logo shape and the signature colour) should be held fixed while everything around them is recalibrated. Restricting the brand colour to the primary action and active state keeps the hue, and arguably sharpens it **[synthesis]**.

With those constants locked, the levers fall into an order of payoff against risk **[synthesis, ordered from case evidence]**. First comes **colour usage, not colour choice**. Regenerate tints and shades from the brand hue in OKLCH or LCH so the steps are perceptually even; Evil Martians' Harmonizer does this with APCA or WCAG contrast targets ([Harmonizer](https://github.com/evilmartians/harmonizer)), and HSL-based ramps produce uneven perceived lightness ([Wildbit](https://www.wildbit.com/blog/accessible-palette-stop-using-hsl-for-color-systems.html)). Then reserve the saturated brand colour for the action and the active state, tint the neutrals slightly toward the brand hue, and take colour off decoration. Second is **chrome versus content**: dim navigation and supporting surfaces, lower border contrast, and drop separators whose job space already does, as Linear did in 2026. Third is **the ink staircase and type within the family**: 3–4 inks with known ratios, 3–5 sizes with one decisive jump, at most three weights, tracking and leading per size, and optical sizes where the family has them. Hierarchy comes from de-emphasising competitors, following Refactoring UI's advice to make labels smaller, lighter and lower in contrast rather than making the primary louder ([Liferay summary](https://liferay.design/articles/2021/diary-of-a-first-year-designer-refactoring-ui/)). Fourth is **spacing rhythm and density**: a set of 5–7 values, with gaps between groups at least twice the gaps inside them. Fifth is **icons**: fewer, smaller, one family. Jakub Krehel's polish rules add concentric radii ("outer radius = inner radius + padding"), shadows for elevation and borders for structure, and optical over geometric alignment ([make-interfaces-feel-better](https://github.com/jakubkrehel/make-interfaces-feel-better/blob/main/skills/make-interfaces-feel-better/SKILL.md)). Sixth is **copy and states**: specific claims, verb-plus-noun actions, and designed empty, loading and error states, all of which change perceived quality without touching identity. **Structure and navigation** come seventh. They have the biggest functional payoff and draw the most pushback, so they go last and behind an opt-in, as at Vercel and Stripe. **Surface material effects** such as glass and blur carry the highest legibility risk of all. NN/g's review of iOS 26 Liquid Glass found it "obscures content instead of letting it take the spotlight" ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/liquid-glass/)†), and Apple has announced a revamp ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquid_Glass)).

Two conflicts come up in almost every fixed-palette redesign, and both have precedents. **A brand colour that fails contrast stays, but changes role.** Keep the hex for non-text marks and large type, and use a darker step from the same perceptual ramp for text. This repo's B5 portfolio did exactly that: it kept the owner's orange `#EC7044` on dark and used `#D4572A` at 3.9:1 on light "non-text only, never on text" ([B5 DESIGN.md](../experiments/runs/taste/b5-minimal/design/DESIGN.md)). taste-skill's rule covers the stronger case: when the brand itself requires a banned device, "keep the brand asset, say so in the report" ([taste-skill](https://github.com/Leonxlnx/taste-skill/blob/main/skills/taste-skill/SKILL.md)). **"More contrast" is not automatically "more accessible."** Twitter's August 2021 high-contrast buttons and Chirp font drew reports of eye strain and headaches, and within about two days Twitter reduced the button contrast ([Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/twitter-tones-down-buttons-eye-strain-154418283.html)). Before planning, the agent should ask what "keep the palette" means. In practice it usually covers the brand hues, while the neutral ramps are free to regenerate. If it means every existing hex is frozen, the remaining levers are proportion and role assignment only **[synthesis]**.

## Ship it in reversible slices: every famous failure was a forced cutover

### Incremental by default, with the big-bang held in reserve

The guidance is unusually unanimous. NN/g says to change incrementally and to resort to a complete redesign only when incremental gains are "miniscule or nonexistent" or when problems are too entangled to fix piecemeal. It also concedes that a radical change can produce a larger jump in quality ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/radical-incremental-redesign/)†). Spool calls the flip-the-switch launch "the most ineffective way to get major changes into a design" ([Center Centre](https://articles.centercentre.com/?p=320)†). Friedman says big-bang redesigns "more often than not … can be avoided with a series of strategic incremental changes" ([Smart Interface Design Patterns](https://smart-interface-design-patterns.com/articles/how-to-redesign-guide/)†) **[consensus]**. Engineering practice has the same shape. Fowler's strangler fig pattern grows the new system around the old one behind a stable boundary, and Thoughtworks applied it to large front-end migrations in which old and new UI ran side by side ([Thoughtworks](https://www.thoughtworks.com/en-th/insights/articles/farewell-angularjs)).

The rollout pattern that worked is **opt-in, then default with opt-out, then mandatory, with dates announced in advance**. GitHub's 2023 navigation went to public beta on 5 April, default-on but switchable on 15 June (after accessibility fixes driven by feedback), and general availability on 17 October, about six months in all ([GitHub changelog](https://github.blog/changelog/2023-04-05-redesigned-navigation-available-in-public-beta/)†; [June](https://github.blog/changelog/2023-06-15-redesigned-navigation-enabled-for-all-users-beta/)†; [October](https://github.blog/changelog/2023-10-17-redesigned-navigation-now-available-to-all-users/)†). Gmail ran a similar six-month schedule in 2018, with a fixed end date for the opt-out ([Google Workspace Updates](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2018/06/new-gmail-timeline.html)). Vercel's dashboard navigation went from an opt-in beta in January to default on 26 February 2026 ([Vercel changelog](https://vercel.com/changelog/dashboard-navigation-redesign-rollout)†). Smaller changes get shorter windows: GitHub's repository page was opt-in for two weeks ([GitHub blog](https://github.blog/news-insights/a-new-look-for-repositories/)†). **The size of the change sets the length of the window** **[consensus]**.

### In code: refactor and restyle are separate commits

Mature design systems migrate in a fixed order **[consensus]**. They introduce semantic tokens first. Atlassian applies and tests tokens across an experience before releasing new colours, uses codemods that "only provide token suggestions" and need manual review, enforces the result with ESLint rules against hard-coded and deprecated tokens, and treats fallbacks as scaffolding to delete when the migration ends ([Atlassian](https://atlassian.design/foundations/tokens/migrate-to-tokens)). They commit mechanical changes apart from manual ones. Shopify Polaris runs each codemod, commits the automated changes, makes hand fixes in a second commit, and leaves grep-able markers where automation was unsafe ([Polaris v11→v12](https://polaris-react.shopify.com/version-guides/migrating-from-v11-to-v12)). They gate component swaps behind flags; GitHub Primer reads them with a `useFeatureFlag` hook, and tests can snapshot both states ([Primer React feature flags](https://tessl.io/registry/skills/github/primer/react/feature-flags)). And they review visual diffs explicitly. Visual-regression tools turn every pixel change into an accept or deny decision, and a restyle produces a lot of them: one public Storybook build showed **1,373 unreviewed changes** ([Chromatic](https://www.chromatic.com/build?appId=635781f3500dd2c49e189caf&number=27267)).

The resulting rule for an agent is **[synthesis]**: map the new tokens to the *current* values first, so the screenshot diff is zero. Then change the values in a separate commit. "Refactor" and "restyle" can then be reviewed and reverted independently. Routes, URLs, form field names, keyboard shortcuts, analytics event names, `data-testid`s and ARIA roles are contracts. Existing end-to-end tests should pass unchanged.

### Measure before, and read week one with suspicion

"Better" can't be shown, and regressions can't be caught, without a baseline taken before the change. HEART supplies the categories: happiness, engagement, adoption, retention and task success, each derived from a goal ([HEART](https://research.google.com/pubs/archive/36299.pdf)†). Short A/B tests of redesigns are biased in both directions. **Novelty** inflates early gains. **Primacy**, the cost of relearning, depresses early results and then recovers. "If your treatment effect varies over time, the naive mean lift is biased for the long-run effect" ([Sadeghi et al. 2021, Microsoft](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2102.12893.pdf)) **[measured]**. The remedy is a long-running holdout. Reddit kept **10% of logged-out users** on the old design, and with old.reddit as a permanent opt-out its daily opt-out rate among logged-in users held near **0.29%** ([r/beta admin post](https://reddit.birdcat.cafe/r/beta/comments/9iu1ax/we_conducted_an_opt_out_survey_on_new_reddit_here)).

Google's change-aversion work treats the negative short-term reaction as expected and manageable through advance notice, a way to switch back, and visible responsiveness to feedback. Google says change aversion "isn't an excuse for worsening user experience" ([Sedley & Müller 2013](https://research.google/pubs/minimizing-change-aversion-for-the-google-drive-launch/)†; [Sedley talk notes](https://tianpan.co/notes/128-change-aversion)). The working distinction is between aversion and regression **[consensus]**. Complaints that fade while behaviour recovers are aversion. Complaints that name a lost capability, a broken task or an accessibility problem, or that coincide with behaviour that stays down, are regression. Instagram's 2022 pause fits the second pattern: "people are frustrated and the usage data isn't great" ([Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/instagram-full-screen-feed-recommended-posts-185859342.html)).

### What the failures cost, and the rule each one teaches

| Case | What happened | Measured cost | Rule for an agent |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Digg v4**, Aug 2010 | Bury button removed; publishers auto-submitted via RSS; forced on everyone | Hitwise: UK traffic **−34%**, US visits **−26%** in a month ([Computerworld](https://www.computerworld.com/article/1515228/users-drop-digg-after-unpopular-redesign.html)); 25 of 67 staff laid off ([ProductMint](https://productmint.com/what-happened-to-digg)) | Never remove a core control without sign-off; the core task must stay as easy or get easier |
| **Snapchat**, 2017–18 | Friends' Stories merged with chat; publishers split off; shipped "despite metric concerns" | 1.2M-signature petition; Q2 2018 DAU fell to **188M**, Snap's first decline, which Spiegel blamed on the redesign ([inkl/Guardian](https://www.inkl.com/news/shares-in-snapchat-owner-plummet-as-redesign-hits-results); [Android Headlines](https://www.androidheadlines.com/2018/05/ceo-pushed-snapchat-redesign-despite-internal-warnings-report.html)) | If before/after measures regress, stop and report; don't rationalise |
| **Skype**, 2017 | Stories-style Highlights, colourful themes; "calling became harder to execute" | Rolled back in Sept 2018 ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2018/09/03/skype-rolls-back-its-redesign-by-ditching-stories-squiggles-and-over-the-top-color)) | The redesign's agenda never outranks the user's job |
| **Sonos app**, May 2024 | Replaced the old app before feature parity; sleep timer and queue editing missing; screen-reader access broken | **$20–30M** fix effort; fiscal Q4 revenue **−16%**; CEO stepped down Jan 2025 ([CX Dive](https://www.customerexperiencedive.com/news/sonos-ceo-departs-botched-app-roll-out/737177/)) | Feature parity and accessibility regressions are failure-grade; never replace in place |
| **Twitter**, Aug 2021 | Extreme button contrast and new font sold as accessibility | Eye-strain reports; contrast reduced within ~2 days ([Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/twitter-tones-down-buttons-eye-strain-154418283.html)) | Test contrast extremes and font rendering across platforms |
| **Instagram**, Jul 2022 | Full-screen feed, run as a *test* | Paused cheaply ([CNN](https://us.cnn.com/2022/07/28/tech/instagram-walks-back-updates)) | Make rollback cheap; ship behind a flag |
| **Spotify**, 2019 | Library features stripped to make room for Podcasts | Reportedly rolled back after users threatened to leave; one outlet, unconfirmed ([Digital Music News](https://www.digitalmusicnews.com/2019/06/21/spotify-axes-mobile-features/)) | Moving or hiding a feature counts as removing it |

The pattern across these cases is that **rollback speed separates an embarrassing week from a CEO departure**, and every recovery came from restoring what was removed, not from waiting out the backlash. Facebook's 2006 News Feed is the counter-case. The feature survived because the company quickly fixed the real defect (missing privacy controls) instead of reverting the concept ([Harvard Crimson](https://thecrimson.com/article/2006/9/11/facebookcom-news-feeds-cause-dissent-in)). The failure literature reduces to one hard gate **[synthesis from cases]**: **a feature-parity inventory**. Every route, control, setting, shortcut and visible field must be present and reachable in no more steps than before. Anything removed or moved is listed for a human to approve.

## Practitioners and AI skills converge on audit, stop, then change in a fixed order

### Emil Kowalski: taste is written-down reasons, and the user chooses

Emil Kowalski's essay "Agents with Taste" could be read only through search snippets†. It reportedly argues that "almost every 'taste' decision has a logical reason if you look close enough", so taste can be encoded for agents as precise rules ([emilkowal.ski](https://emilkowal.ski/ui/agents-with-taste)†). His skills repository, which he describes as his blog articles turned into skills, was read in full. It describes taste as "a trained instinct", and says that "most details users never consciously notice. That is the point" ([emil-design-eng](https://github.com/emilkowalski/skills/blob/main/skills/emil-design-eng/SKILL.md)). He has no "redesign the page" skill. His skills cover motion and detail. But his audit workflow is the most transferable improvement model found, and it maps directly onto redesign.

**`improve-animations`** is the audit workflow. It starts with recon of the project's existing conventions ("plans must extend these, not invent parallel ones"), its personality, and a frequency map that "drives severity". It then vets every finding: "Never present a finding you haven't confirmed at its file:line". It ranks findings by leverage (impact ÷ effort), lists missed opportunities separately because they are additive rather than corrective, and then "**stop[s] and wait[s] for the user to select**". It never modifies source code. "Don't re-litigate settled decisions" protects documented trade-offs. "'The motion here is already right' is a valid audit result." Each plan carries explicit boundaries ("Do NOT change markup/structure") and a mandatory feel check ([improve-animations](https://github.com/emilkowalski/skills/blob/main/skills/improve-animations/SKILL.md); [PLAN-TEMPLATE](https://github.com/emilkowalski/skills/blob/main/skills/improve-animations/PLAN-TEMPLATE.md)).

**`remove-animations`** reverses the burden of proof: "'It's already there' and 'it looks nice' are not defenses". It also warns that "deleting everything is also wrong" ([remove-animations](https://github.com/emilkowalski/skills/blob/main/skills/remove-animations/SKILL.md)).

**`prototype`** answers broad requests with divergent variants of "one thing per run", the single highest-leverage piece. Variants diverge "on a named axis" and share the project's tokens, because "sharing the project's tokens is not convergence". Each variant states when it is the right choice and what it costs, and the skill presents them and stops: "the choice belongs to the user" ([prototype](https://github.com/emilkowalski/skills/blob/main/skills/prototype/SKILL.md)).

**Reviews** use a mandatory Before | After | Why table ([emil-design-eng](https://github.com/emilkowalski/skills/blob/main/skills/emil-design-eng/SKILL.md)).

Read across these skills, Emil treats improvement as three distinct jobs: **corrective** (fix what is there), **subtractive** (remove what shouldn't be) and **additive** (supply what is missing). A redesign report should keep those three apart **[consensus]**.

The other polish practitioners add guardrails a redesign skill should copy. Jakub Krehel: "identify the project's existing styling system and express the change in that system … Never introduce a second styling system just to apply a polish fix". He also asks for a **"Considered but Rejected"** table, and says "never pad the report" ([make-interfaces-feel-better](https://github.com/jakubkrehel/make-interfaces-feel-better/blob/main/skills/make-interfaces-feel-better/SKILL.md)). Rauno Freiberg's interface guidelines are detail rules, such as "font weight should not change on hover or selected state to prevent layout shift" ([raunofreiberg/interfaces](https://github.com/raunofreiberg/interfaces/blob/main/README.md)). Vercel's agent version reports findings by file:line with high signal-to-noise ([vercel-labs/web-interface-guidelines](https://github.com/vercel-labs/web-interface-guidelines/blob/main/command.md)). All four share three habits **[consensus]**: changes go inside the incumbent system; findings are tied to a location; and restraint is proven, through "already right", "considered but rejected" and "never pad".

### What the AI design skills prescribe, and where they disagree

The mature skills share one sequence **[consensus]**: record the before state, classify the request, audit with evidence, stop for a review, get the user's priorities, apply changes in a low-risk order, then verify against the before and smoke-test every flow that worked.

| Skill | Distinctive redesign rule |
|---|---|
| **Impeccable** v4.8 | "Refinement preserves; redesign replaces." "Established world: inherit it. A missing DESIGN.md does not erase a coherent identity already in code." Its critique runs the design judgement *before* detector output enters, because detector output "still anchors judgment". It ends with questions tied to findings, including "Should any sections stay as-is?" "Never auto-run a command." ([SKILL.md](https://github.com/pbakaus/impeccable/blob/main/.devin/skills/impeccable/SKILL.md); [critique.md](https://github.com/pbakaus/impeccable/blob/main/.devin/skills/impeccable/reference/critique.md); [routing.md](https://github.com/pbakaus/impeccable/blob/main/.devin/skills/impeccable/reference/routing.md)) |
| **taste-skill** v2 | "Record the before." "A review stops here … name the three changes that would move the page most." No framework migration. Order of work: type, colour, frame, states, then stock components. "Prove it": before/after at every width, with every link, form and menu clicked ([taste-skill](https://github.com/Leonxlnx/taste-skill/blob/main/skills/taste-skill/SKILL.md)) |
| **taste-skill classics** | Mode misclassification is the biggest failure. "What Never Changes Silently": URL slugs, primary nav labels, form field names and order, logo, legal copy. | **ux-ui-agent-skills** `redesign` | Scan, diagnose, direct, apply, verify. "Tokens first … This alone fixes most consistency slop." | **Superdesign** | "Reproduce first, then branch variations." "Reproduction prompt = structure, not aesthetic": adjectives plus thin context render "a generic marketing page instead of your real page." Logo and icons reproduced 1:1 ([superdesign-skill](https://github.com/superdesigndev/superdesign-skill)) |

| **Anthropic frontend-design** | No redesign protocol. "The brief's own words always win" ([Claude blog](https://claude.com/blog/improving-frontend-design-through-skills)) |

They disagree in two places. ux-ui-agent-skills puts **tokens first** and taste-skill puts **type first**. These are reconcilable: the token layer is the vehicle and type is the first value to change through it. The other disagreement is whether a broad request deserves one rebuild or **several variants**. Emil and Superdesign favour variants, and the rest assume one direction. The failure modes the authors encode are consistent and serve as indirect evidence of what agents get wrong in practice: a generic page replacing the real one, invented logos and icons, split palettes across pages, broken slugs, nav labels and form names, polish that smuggles in a redesign, re-litigated settled decisions, a thin stripped page, a second styling system, and `// rest unchanged` partial output. User reports are thin but match ("it changed some things. Not the right things"; [DEV snippet](https://dev.to/yuki-uix/i-asked-ai-to-fix-my-ui-20-times-the-problem-wasnt-my-prompts-527j)†). None of these protocols has published outcome data.

### What this repo's own runs add

The current taste skill has two paths: a fast path for small edits when `design/DESIGN.md` exists, and the full greenfield process for "anything the user calls a redesign" ([SKILL.md](../skills/taste/SKILL.md)). Steps 1 to 4 of that process (ask what sites you love, measure a reference, diverge five directions and drop the most likely, write a token plan) assume a blank slate. The critic compares the build with the reference and with the best-so-far version, never with the before. The two real-project runs show where that works and where it doesn't.

**B4's follow-ups were redesigns in all but name.** The dark-theme follow-up was a restyle on a kept layout. The mobile follow-up was a set of targeted fixes. Both were done ad hoc, keeping `index.before-*.html` copies and a before/after PNG beside the work, which is an informal "record the before" ([results.md](../experiments/results.md)).

**B5 was a real redesign of the owner's live portfolio**, and it succeeded through the greenfield path for reasons the evidence predicts. It also kept the incumbent's constants without being told to: the orange period `#EC7044` "from the current site", Geist as "the owner's current face", and the B4 dark ground ([B5 DESIGN.md](../experiments/runs/taste/b5-minimal/design/DESIGN.md)). It succeeded because the owner explicitly asked for a structural replacement, and a personal portfolio has few habituated users. Those are exactly the conditions under which a radical redesign is defensible. The critic's round-1 P0 (inks reversed against the reference, so the paragraph outweighed the name) was a calibration error, not a structural one. And after launch the owner refined the site in small iterations (a theme switch, an icon, the CV removed, link previews), which is the incremental model in miniature.

The lesson **[synthesis]** is that the greenfield process is right for an owner-directed overhaul of a low-traffic page. It is the wrong default for "make this better" on a product people use daily, which is the case a separate skill should catch.

## A staged procedure for taste-redesign

`taste-redesign` should live at `skills/taste-redesign/` in the same plugin. The plugin manifest's `source: "./"` already discovers sibling skill folders, so no manifest change is needed beyond a version bump. It should call the taste scripts by relative path (`<skill>/../taste/scripts/probe.mjs`) and read taste's library and references in place, so there is one copy of each. Routing has to change at the same time. The earlier report found that colliding skill descriptions average into "different but ugly" ([Giving AI UI design taste](<Giving AI UI design taste.md>)). So taste's description should stop claiming "redesign, restyle or polish" of existing UI and hand those requests to `taste-redesign`, keeping only its fast path for small edits to a design it already planned. `taste-redesign` in turn hands owner-directed overhauls and rebrands back to taste's full process once the before state and the must-survive list are recorded.

The new scripts should import taste's `lib/browser.mjs` instead of duplicating browser handling. `inventory` can start from the profile `probe` already writes, which has shares per family, weight and size. The skill should be validated the way taste was, with a blind pairwise test on a real existing page. Take the B4-dark portfolio as the before, with the brief "make it better, keep the palette, fonts and logo". Compare three arms: no skill, taste's greenfield process, and `taste-redesign` in restyle mode. Score with the owner's blind pick plus the parity and compare reports. Until that test runs, the procedure is a well-grounded proposal, not a measured improvement.

## Conclusion

In a redesign, the incumbent page is two things at once. Its constants (logo, brand hue, faces, labels, routes) are the best-measured reference the product has, and they should be inherited the way taste inherits a reference's numbers. Its drift (85 greys, 11 font sizes, colour on decoration, nothing leading) is the anti-reference. So most of the taste in a redesign lies in deciding what *not* to change, then recalibrating the relationships between what stays. That makes a redesign skill mainly a preservation-and-subtraction machine with a narrow additive budget. It is the opposite of the greenfield skill's job, which is to invent decisions the average web lacks, and that is the real reason the two should be separate skills sharing one toolset rather than one skill with a branch.

The evidence also changes what the agent should claim. An AI audit is a single evaluator, and in human studies a single evaluator found about a third of the problems. Week-one reactions are biased both ways. The costliest failures came from removal and irreversibility, not from bad taste. An agent therefore earns trust less by producing a striking new look than by producing three artefacts its user can check: a before state that was measured, a parity report showing nothing was lost, and a rollout that can be reversed. The two open questions that matter most are both testable cheaply in this repo. First, does an audit-first staged restyle beat a greenfield rerun on a page that already exists? Second, what counts of greys, sizes and spacings separate a calibrated product from a drifted one? No published benchmark exists for the second, and the probe can produce one from the library already in hand.
