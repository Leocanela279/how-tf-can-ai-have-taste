# Prove the work fast, then earn every icon

A good portfolio is a **typical structure carrying unusual proof**. Reviewers make a keep-reading decision in seconds and give a site a few minutes at most, so the page has to put its claim, its evidence and a way to make contact where people expect them. The defensible skeleton is the one admired engineer sites already share: name, role and proof on the first screen, then 3–5 selected projects, a dated experience list, a short human About section, an easy-to-copy email, and a footer. Card grids are not required by any of it. The sites people point to use typographic lists, dated indexes, spec-like entries and timelines, and the more exotic the container, the more the site has to rely on its owner's fame. The owner's own portfolio already avoids the card grid. Its weakness is that six projects share one template and each leads with a screenshot of the product's surface, while the stated pitch is the invisible layer underneath. Its new colophon footer meets nearly every point in the footer evidence. What remains is detail work of the kind Apple does: a page-level landmark, copy-to-clipboard for the email, consistent external-link marks, and a colophon whose claims are strictly true. On icons the evidence is unusually clear. Outside a handful of universal glyphs, **text carries meaning and icons only speed up scanning of repeated items**. An envelope next to "Email" is decoration. A library does not make an app distinctive; restraint and tuning do. Byte costs are trivial, and the real performance traps are barrel imports, dynamic icon loaders and unsubsetted icon fonts.

Evidence labels used throughout: **[M]** measured (eye-tracking, task logs, benchmarks), **[S]** survey of reviewers, **[G]** official guideline from a platform, standards body or established research group, **[O]** practitioner opinion, **[Obs]** what a live site or component showed when fetched on 2026-10-29, **[V]** vendor claim with no stated method, **[R]** first-hand evidence from this repository, **[I]** this report's inference.

## Reviewers decide in seconds, so the skeleton must be the typical one

No one has published an eye-tracking study of portfolio websites. The closest measured data is about résumés. In Ladders' 2018 eye-tracking study, recruiters spent **7.4 seconds** on an initial screen. Simple layouts with clear sections and headings held attention, while clutter, multiple columns and missing headers did worse **[M]** ([HR Dive](https://www.hrdive.com/news/eye-tracking-study-shows-recruiters-look-at-resumes-for-7-seconds/541582/); [Ladders PDF](https://csuglobal.edu/sites/default/files/blog-files/theladders-eyetracking-studyc2.pdf)). Critics point out that this number measures a filter decision (keep reading or not) rather than an evaluation, and that the sample was small ([ERE](https://www.ere.net/articles/is-the-6-second-resume-scan-a-myth)). Read correctly, that criticism is the useful part. The first screen decides only *whether* anyone evaluates the work.

Surveys describe the evaluation itself. NN/g surveyed **204 UX hiring professionals**. Hiring managers very rarely read a whole portfolio, and they want process rather than polish: the problem, your role, constraints, iterations, the decisions you made and the value delivered. NN/g recommends **3–5 detailed case studies**, quality over quantity, all on one consistent template **[S]** ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-design-portfolios/)). A report attributed to UXfolio's "State of UX Portfolios 2026" claims that 65% of reviewers spend under four minutes on a first look. Its top red flags are low visual quality (55%), confusing structure (48%), obvious overuse of AI (43%) and overly dense content (42%). Only a LinkedIn excerpt of this report could be found, so treat it as unconfirmed **[S, unverified]** ([LinkedIn snippet](https://bg.linkedin.com/in/kalin-zdravkov)). A head of product design gives similar practitioner benchmarks: about 5 minutes for a whole portfolio, about 20 minutes for a full case study. His red flags include portfolios that all look the same and a text-only "boring" About page **[O]** ([ADPList](https://adplist.org/blog/what-is-a-hiring-manager-looking-for-in-a-design-portfolio)).

For engineers the site matters less than the proof it points to. In Profy.dev's survey of about 60 React hiring managers, 93% said they were likely to look at a junior candidate's portfolio site, yet **51% said not having one would not lower the candidate's chances** **[S, small, c. 2020]** ([DEV summary](https://dev.to/profydev/this-survey-among-60-hiring-managers-reveals-don-t-waste-your-time-on-a-react-portfolio-website-17ge)). In HackerRank's older executive survey, 84.1% named GitHub project work as an important signal of skill **[S, dated]** ([ITPro Today](https://itprotoday.com/it-management/developers-show-your-github-project-to-attract-job-offers)). The conclusion is that an engineer's site is a **routing layer to proof**: it pays off when it makes shipped products, repositories and numbers quick to reach and believable, and adds nothing when it is only a showpiece **[I]**.

Admired engineer sites converge on the same first screen. brittanychiang.com, paco.me, emilkowal.ski and leerob.com each open with a name, a role, a one-line claim and a **recognizable affiliation** in the first lines (Linear, Vercel, Klaviyo, Next.js). They feature **3 or 4 projects**, which falls inside NN/g's range **[Obs]** ([paco.me](https://paco.me); [emilkowal.ski](https://emilkowal.ski); [brittanychiang.com](https://brittanychiang.com); [leerob.com](https://leerob.com)). Brittany Chiang's site adds a dated experience list (year range, role, short description, plain-text tech tags) and links to a full archive, so the home page stays short without hiding anything **[Obs]** ([brittanychiang.com](https://brittanychiang.com)). No controlled evidence compares single-page and multi-page portfolios. The common answer, a scannable index with depth one click away, is convention **[Obs/O]**.

This repo's blind test fits that pattern. The owner chose the richer, conventional portfolio (sticky nav, meta strip, live clock, focus strip, project index) over a sparse, structurally novel one, even though the deterministic gate scored his choice worse **[R]** ([results.md](../experiments/results.md)). The earlier research explains why. Low complexity combined with high prototypicality wins first impressions, and novelty adds appeal only while the design still reads as typical (Tuch 2012; Hekkert's MAYA) **[M]** ([Tuch et al.](https://research.google/pubs/the-role-of-visual-complexity-and-prototypicality-regarding-first-impression-of-websites-working-towards-understanding-aesthetic-judgments/); [Hekkert et al.](https://research.vu.nl/en/publications/most-advanced-yet-acceptable-typicality-and-novelty-as-joint-pred/)). There is one more point the minimalist exemplars hide. paco.me and emilkowal.ski can afford three bare links because their owners' names and projects already supply the proof. An engineer without that recognition who copies the sparseness loses the proof layer as well. So an agent should default to *richer* proof (dates, role, outcome, a real artifact) unless the person is well known **[I]**.

## The card grid is one container among six, and the list is the admired default

The card grid did not come from admired portfolios. It is the default output of templates and models. Practitioner catalogs of AI tells name the identical icon-plus-heading-plus-text card grid as the standard generated homepage **[O]** ([Impeccable](https://924b8765.impeccable-2rv.pages.dev/slop); [devtake](https://devtake.pages.dev/article/ai-generated-frontend-slop)). The engineer sites people admire barely use cards. Their dominant pattern is a **typographic list**: linked name, one sentence, a single column, sometimes a year **[Obs]** ([paco.me](https://paco.me); [emilkowal.ski](https://emilkowal.ski)). The other alternatives observed fall into the families below. No study compares their effect on hiring outcomes, so choosing among them is a matter of fit, not proof **[I]**.

| Container | Seen at | Fits when | Cost or risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Typographic list (name, one-liner, optional year or role) | paco.me, emilkowal.ski **[Obs]** | Default for engineers: fastest scan, highest density | Proves little unless the projects are famous; add role, year or a metric |
| Dated experience list + 3–4 featured projects with image and metric + archive link | brittanychiang.com **[Obs]** | "Rich and conventional", the pattern the B4 result favoured | More writing to do and keep up to date |
| Index or table with hover preview (no. · title · client · year) | Framer components "Project Hover List", "ReelIndex", "Hover Project List" **[Obs]** ([Framer](https://www.framer.com/marketplace/components/project-hover-list/); [ReelIndex](https://www.framer.com/marketplace/components/reelindex/); [Hover Project List](https://www.framer.com/marketplace/components/hover-project-list/)) | Visual work, designer or agency feel | Needs an inline-thumbnail fallback on touch, keyboard focus and Escape to close; each row must make sense without its preview |
| Changelog or timeline, newest first | leerob.com dated posts; rauno.me year-archived site versions **[Obs]** ([leerob.com](https://leerob.com); [rauno.me](https://rauno.me)) | The person ships often | Looks thin when updates are rare |
| Ranked or categorized list | joshwcomeau.com's numbered "Popular Content" **[Obs]** ([joshwcomeau.com](https://www.joshwcomeau.com)) | Content-heavy people | Needs enough items to rank |
| Playable world or OS metaphor | bruno-simon.com, daedalOS **[Obs]** ([bruno-simon.com](https://bruno-simon.com); [LinuxLinks](https://linuxlinks.com/daedalos-desktop-environment-web-browser)) | Only when the portfolio itself is the proof (WebGL, systems work) | Very costly to build well; hides content behind interaction; works against the four-minute scan and the "confusing structure" red flag |

A seventh option suits engineers, though this research found no admired site that uses it exactly. It is a **spec-sheet entry**: a README-like block with labeled fields for Role, When, Team, Stack, Outcome and Links. It combines NN/g's required case-study content with the format engineers already read every day **[I]**. The common thread is that all of these move novelty into the *content and typography* while keeping the order a reviewer expects (who, what, proof, contact). That is the MAYA split the earlier research recommends **[I]**.

## Leandro's six projects are card-free already, but one template repeats six times

The current page in [b4-dark/index.html](../experiments/runs/baseline/b4-dark/index.html) does not use a card grid. After the hero, a three-column "schema / access / failure" strip uses numbered mono labels where a template would place three icon tiles. A numbered index (01–06, name, client, year) sits beside the Work heading, and each project is an article with a sticky left column (dates, title, two paragraphs, a stack line, links) beside a large framed screenshot with lettered bullet points **[R]**. That is the dated index plus featured entries pattern, done well. The owner's complaint about "AI slop cards" ([critiques.md](../experiments/runs/baseline/b4-dark/design/critiques.md)) therefore points at what the agent should *not* drift into, rather than at the current page. Three real weaknesses remain **[I]**. First, six entries share one tall template, so the scroll from ICEX Next to Club Stas Tarat reads as six repetitions of the same shape. That is one more than NN/g's upper bound, and two of the six are closely related. Second, the visuals undercut the claim. The hero says he enjoys "the data model, the permissions, and what happens when something fails", yet ICEX Next is shown by its sign-in screen and Reskillia by its marketing headline, the least informative screens either product has. Third, no entry states his role or an outcome. The text says what each product does but not what *he* decided or what changed because of it, and those are exactly the fields NN/g's reviewers ask for **[S]** ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-design-portfolios/)).

Here are five card-free ways to present these specific projects. Each one keeps the typical page order.

**Merge the lineage into one case study.** Reskillia (Mar 2026) and ICEX Next (Sep 2026) are the same codebase, and the ICEX copy already says it was "cut down to what the client needed: no AI, no public sign-up, privacy first." One entry titled *Humannova: one platform, two products* could set the two side by side as a kept-and-removed ledger: Gemini scoring removed, public sign-up replaced by invitations, encrypted personal data and RLS kept, versioned questionnaires and consent records added. This is the decisions-and-abandoned-concepts content NN/g reviewers want, and it takes the list from six entries to five **[S/I]**.

**Show the invisible layer instead of the login screen.** For a backend-weighted engineer, the most persuasive artifact is the thing a screenshot cannot show. For ICEX Next that could be a small typeset table of consent records (user, policy version, accepted at), or a diagram of a draft migrating between questionnaire versions. For Reskillia it is the expected-versus-achieved radar chart the product actually outputs. For db-doctor it is a real excerpt of a generated report set in Geist Mono, which beats the current abstract reads-and-returns diagram. For Tomate it is a crop of the macOS menu bar item, plus a two-line sleep/wake timeline that shows why time is stored as dates. CPU City already has the right visual. A short muted loop of the chip-to-city zoom (poster frame, `preload="none"`, played only when visible, a still frame under reduced motion) would show its one idea better than a still image. Keep product screenshots as secondary images. This option turns the hero's claim into evidence, and it is the most distinctive move available to him **[I]**.

**Turn each entry into a spec sheet.** Keep the sticky left column, but replace the two paragraphs with labeled rows in his mono label style: Role, When, Team, Stack, Hard part, Outcome, Links. Club Stas Tarat already contains the raw material ("almost forty years old, about a hundred members", a team of three). It lacks a line on which parts were his. The owner explicitly chose mono labels over the catalogue's objections ([critiques.md](../experiments/runs/baseline/b4-dark/design/critiques.md)), so this format plays to his taste rather than against it **[R/I]**.

**Make the index the primary surface.** The existing numbered index can become the whole Work section. Each row expands in place, using `<details>`/`<summary>` (no JavaScript, keyboard-accessible), to show the spec rows and one artifact. On pointer devices a preview image can follow hover, gated by `@media (hover: hover) and (pointer: fine)`, with inline thumbnails on touch, as the marketplace components already do **[Obs]** ([Framer](https://www.framer.com/marketplace/components/project-hover-list/)). This shortens the page a great deal and gives the progressive disclosure the ADPList reviewer praises **[O]** ([ADPList](https://adplist.org/blog/what-is-a-hiring-manager-looking-for-in-a-design-portfolio)). The cost is that depth sits behind a click, which matters more for a less-known engineer.

**Add a two-lane timeline as the section header.** His dates are dense and real: Club Stas Tarat from Mar 2025 to Jul 2026, Reskillia from Mar 2026, ICEX Next from Sep 2026, and CPU City, Tomate and db-doctor all in Oct 2026. A thin horizontal axis with a client-work lane and a personal lane (bars for durations, ticks for launches) would show at a glance that he ships in parallel and that his pace is speeding up. Below 640px it becomes a vertical list. It complements the entries rather than replacing them, and it is built entirely from facts already on the page **[I]**.

The recommended combination keeps the current sticky-column rows, because they are the typical structure he picked. It merges Humannova into one lineage entry, puts a spec block (with Role and Outcome) above each set of bullets, and replaces the surface screenshots with artifacts of the invisible layer. That gives five entries, inside the 3–5 range, with novelty in content and type rather than in the layout. The timeline is optional decoration. It is worth adding only if he also wants the page to say "I ship often" **[I]**. Two small changes complete it. Put the stack line *after* the outcome, since NN/g and the developer-portfolio guides both say to lead with the problem, not the technology **[S/O]** ([Scrimba](https://scrimba.com/articles/how-to-build-a-web-developer-portfolio-that-gets-you-hired)). And drop the `rounded-xl border bg-card` frame around images in favour of a hairline or a bare image, so that no artifact reads as a card.

## A footer is a second-chance zone, and his colophon passes nearly every test

NN/g describes the footer as a place people go **on purpose**, for contact, about and social links, or **as a last resort** when they cannot find something. That makes it "a second chance to be convinced". Footers rarely hurt. The failure modes are too much content, vague labels, missing grouping, low contrast and collapsing the footer, because "people expect it to be there" **[G]** ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/footers/)). No measured data exists on footer use for personal sites. The guidance is qualitative.

What looks like care in a footer is a matter of layering and precision, not decoration. Apple's homepage footer stacks numbered footnotes tied to claims made on the page, 11 grouped sitemap columns, a "more ways to shop" contact line, copyright, a row of legal links and a region selector **[Obs]** ([apple.com](https://www.apple.com/)). Every layer does a job, and the footnotes are the telling detail: the footer answers questions the page raised. Personal sites scale this down in recognizable ways. Josh Comeau runs a full doormat footer with personality in its controls (a sounds toggle, dark mode, RSS) **[Obs]** ([joshwcomeau.com](https://www.joshwcomeau.com)). Brittany Chiang ends with a colophon (designed in Figma, built with Next.js and Tailwind, deployed on Vercel, set in Inter) and a playful time-travel link **[Obs]** ([brittanychiang.com](https://brittanychiang.com)). paco.me signs off with a motto and the year, and rauno.me offers a click-to-copy email with a "Copied" state **[Obs]** ([paco.me](https://paco.me); [rauno.me](https://rauno.me)). Gallery and component work adds the award-site moves: a live clock with the visitor's time offset, an availability dot, a name set at full viewport width **[Obs]** ([footer.design](https://www.footer.design/); [21st.dev Sign-off Footer](https://21st.dev/@ham7a311/components/sign-off-footer.md)). Those are decoration on top of NN/g's functional baseline. They should never replace it.

The new footer in [index.html](../experiments/runs/baseline/b4-dark/index.html) has a name with a sign-off line that echoes the hero ("I build the part nobody sees, so the part they do keeps working"), a Work column linking all six projects, an Elsewhere column (GitHub, GitLab, CV, Email), a Colophon definition list (Type, Built with, Cookies, Barcelona time), and a bottom rule with © year and "Back to top" **[R]**. Measured against the evidence, it is strong. The table below lists what holds up and what does not.

| Check | Evidence | His footer | Verdict and fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| Visible, grouped, clearly labeled, never collapsed | NN/g **[G]** | Four labeled groups on the page's 12-column grid; nothing collapses | Pass |
| Legible small type | WCAG 4.5:1 **[G]** | Labels at `ondeep/60` measure **5.88:1** on `#1A1915`; body ink **14.4:1**; dividers at 15% and 10% are decorative **[R, computed]** | Pass. Keep labels at 60% or above; 50% drops to 4.47:1 |
| Targets | WCAG 2.5.8, 24px minimum **[G]** | Footer links are `inline-block` with 3px block padding on a 22.4px line, about 28px tall **[R]** | Pass |
| Doormat navigation | NN/g; Josh Comeau **[G/Obs]** | Lists the projects rather than the top-level sections | Pass; deeper than global nav, which suits a one-page site |
| Colophon | Brittany Chiang **[Obs]** | Type, build, cookies, local time | Pass, with two truth problems (below) |
| Sign-off / human touch | paco.me, 21st.dev **[Obs]** | Sign-off line plus Barcelona time | Pass; the clock duplicates the hero meta strip |
| Easy-to-copy email | rauno.me, 21st.dev **[Obs]** | `mailto:` only, about 200px below the giant mailto link | **Add** a copy button with a "Copied" state; mailto fails for people without a configured mail client |
| Page landmark | HTML: `<footer>` maps to `contentinfo` only when it is not inside `main`, `section`, `article`, `aside` or `nav` **[G]** ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/HTML/Element/footer)) | The `<footer>` sits inside `<section id="contact">` inside `<main>` **[R]** | **Fix.** Close `main` before the footer, keeping the dark band continuous by wrapping both in a neutral `div`, so screen-reader users get a real page footer. This is the "hardware nobody sees" |
| Consistent link marks | Internal consistency **[I]** | Project links elsewhere carry ↗ for "leaves the site"; the footer's GitHub, GitLab and CV links open new tabs with no mark | **Fix**: add ↗ to external footer links, or state the rule and drop it everywhere |
| Claims that are strictly true | Engineers read colophons as quality claims **[I]** | "Built with HTML and Tailwind CSS" ships the Tailwind Play CDN, which the earlier probe measured at **123 KB gzip, render-blocking** **[R]** ([Fast and beautiful UI](Fast%20and%20beautiful%20UI.md)). "No tracking" sits next to fonts loaded from Google's servers | **Fix**: compile Tailwind at build time and self-host the fonts, then the colophon can say "Tailwind CSS, compiled; fonts self-hosted; 0 third-party requests", a claim a visitor can check in DevTools |

What would take it from good to Apple-level is the same rule Apple's footnotes follow: **the footer answers questions the page raised** **[I]**. The page claims he cares about failure and privacy, and the colophon can prove that about the site itself: zero third-party requests, page weight, and "works without JavaScript" (the reveal fallback already does). The duplicated clock can become availability ("Open to backend roles from …") or "Updated Oct 2026", generated at build time. Small typographic precision belongs here as well. Keep tabular figures for the time and year, align the label column of the definition list, use the same underline motion as the rest of the site, and bring the CV entry in line with the hero's "CV" pill, so that every name for the same thing matches. None of this costs measurable performance. All of it is the detail that is noticed only when it is missing.

## Icons: labels carry meaning, icons speed scanning of repeated items

The best-supported finding on icons is that **almost none are universally understood**. NN/g's usability work lists home, print and the search magnifier, with the hamburger becoming known. Hearts and stars change meaning from site to site, and a clock icon for "history" went unclicked by every test participant. Its rule is to give icons a visible text label, never one that appears only on hover, and to drop any concept a designer cannot draw within five seconds **[G, based on testing]** ([NN/g Icon Usability](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/icon-usability/)). The measured costs of leaning on icons are concrete. In NN/g's hidden-navigation study (179 participants, 6 sites), desktop users opened navigation hidden behind an icon in **27%** of cases, against **48–50%** when it was visible. Hidden navigation made tasks at least **39% slower** on desktop, and content discoverability dropped by **more than 20%** on both desktop and mobile **[M]** ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/hamburger-menus/)). NN/g showed 107 people the "AI" sparkles icon on its own, and **no one** associated it with AI. Answers split between favouriting and visual effects **[M]** ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ai-sparkles-icon-problem/)). Wiedenbeck's 1999 study found icon-only buttons clearly worse for novices in the first session, with the gap narrowing but retention still weaker later. That fits the pattern that icons cost most for new or infrequent users. This research saw only a summary of that study **[M, secondary]** ([Scilit](https://scilit.com/publications/69a8ee4f1d6cf726a650945daf8a929a)). The opposing view, that well-known icons on mobile can go without labels, is a reasonable opinion about a narrow case **[O]** ([UX Movement](https://uxmovement.com/mobile/why-you-shouldnt-always-label-your-icons/)).

**Can icons replace redundant text?** Only in three cases: the few universal glyphs (close, search, home, print, and menu on mobile); dense toolbars used by repeat experts (bold, italic, undo, row actions); and space-starved controls that carry tooltips and an accessible name. Everywhere else the text is what carries the meaning, and removing it trades a measurable comprehension cost for a few pixels **[G/M/I]**. **Is an envelope next to "Email" redundant?** Yes. The label already says everything, the icon adds nothing a reader needs, and an icon on every label is the decoration NN/g warns turns into visual noise **[G]** ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/icon-usability/)). It earns a place only in a *repeated* list of channels that are scanned as a set (a contact row of six services), where shape helps the eye find the right row. A functional icon next to the email, such as a copy glyph that turns into a check, does work: it tells the user what will happen. No study isolates a redundant icon against label-only text for scan speed, so this verdict rests on guidelines and judgement, not measurement **[I]**.

**Are icon libraries important, do you need one, and will one make an app unique?** A library matters in proportion to how many actions a product has. For an app with dozens of actions, one consistent set provides drawn coverage, a shared grid and accessible defaults. Lucide, for example, ships `aria-hidden="true"` and recommends putting the accessible name on the button **[G/V]** ([Lucide](https://lucide.dev/guide/advanced/accessibility)). A portfolio needs perhaps three glyphs, so a few inline SVGs, or the Unicode arrows the owner already uses, are enough. No library makes an app unique. The generic look *is* a library default: Lucide at stroke 2, inside a tinted rounded square, above every heading. Practitioner catalogs name exactly that "icon tile stacked above heading" as the standard AI feature card **[O]** ([Impeccable](https://924b8765.impeccable-2rv.pages.dev/slop)). Distinctiveness comes from **using fewer icons**, **matching their weight to the type**, choosing a less default set or weight, and drawing a few brand-critical glyphs by hand **[I]**. Apple's own system shows what "matched to the type" means. SF Symbols comes in nine weights that pair with the nine San Francisco font weights, at three scales defined relative to cap height, so symbols "automatically align with text" **[G]** ([Apple HIG, SF Symbols](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/sf-symbols)). The HIG also asks for consistent size, detail and stroke across all icons, icon weight matched to the adjacent text, and **optical nudges** for asymmetric glyphs, adjustments that are "typically very small, but they can have a big impact" **[G]** ([Apple HIG, Icons](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/icons)). Material Symbols puts this into a variable font: an optical-size axis (20–48) thickens strokes as icons shrink, and a grade axis reduces glare for light-on-dark **[G]** ([Google Fonts](https://developers.google.com/fonts/docs/material_symbols)). SF Symbols itself cannot be used on the web or in logos under Apple's terms **[G]** ([Apple HIG](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/sf-symbols)).

The mainstream React sets are all permissively licensed (Lucide ISC; Heroicons, Phosphor, Tabler and Radix MIT), and none requires attribution **[O/V]** ([DEV](https://dev.to/usapopopooon/what-i-didnt-know-about-icon-library-licenses-and-you-might-not-either-30of); [Radix](https://github.com/radix-ui/icons)). They differ in look. Lucide is a 24px outline at stroke 2 and the shadcn-era default. Heroicons comes as 24px outline/solid plus a 20px mini set, with about 300 icons. Phosphor has six weights. Tabler has the largest outline catalogue, around 6,000. Radix is a crisp 15×15 grid for dense UI **[V]** ([PkgPulse](https://www.pkgpulse.com/guides/lucide-vs-heroicons-vs-phosphor-react-icon-libraries-2026); [svgicons.com](https://svgicons.com/articles/lucide-vs-tabler-vs-phosphor-icons)). For an agent, the first move toward a less generic look is to change Lucide's defaults and use fewer icons, not to switch libraries **[I]**.

**What do they cost, and how should you approach it?** Bytes are almost never the problem. A tree-shaken icon is roughly **0.4–1 KB raw**, and 50 icons come to about 7–15 KB gzipped depending on the set **[V]** ([PkgPulse](https://www.pkgpulse.com/guides/lucide-vs-heroicons-vs-phosphor-react-icon-libraries-2026)). One lucide-react module (`house.js`) is about 600 characters, mostly path data passed to a shared factory **[M, approximate]** ([unpkg](https://unpkg.com/lucide-react@0.460.0/dist/esm/icons/house.js)). The real costs sit elsewhere. Barrel imports slow builds. Vercel measured lucide-react compiling **1,583 modules before and 333 after** `optimizePackageImports`, with dev boot dropping from **5.8 s to 3 s**, and `@material-ui/icons` going from **11,738 to 632 modules** **[M]** ([Vercel](https://vercel.com/blog/how-we-optimized-package-imports-in-next-js)). Name-string or dynamic icon loaders pull in the whole set **[I]**. Phosphor components carry all six weights each, so tree-shaking works per icon, not per weight **[V]** ([PkgPulse](https://www.pkgpulse.com/guides/lucide-vs-heroicons-vs-phosphor-react-icon-libraries-2026)). Icon fonts are the biggest trap. The full Material Symbols variable font is **7.9 MB**, the default axes are **295 KB**, and a three-icon subset is **1.7 KB**. Without `display=block`, ligature words like "home" briefly show up as text **[M, vendor]** ([Google Fonts](https://developers.google.com/fonts/docs/material_symbols)). Icon fonts also render as anti-aliased text with poor semantics, while SVG declares itself an image **[O]** ([CSS-Tricks](https://css-tricks.com/?p=168417)). The last cost is SVG shipped inside JavaScript, which adds parse and execution time. When icons repeat often, a `<symbol>` sprite referenced with `<use>` keeps `currentColor` working and avoids that cost; pulling one icon from a 100 KB sprite does not **[O]** ([kurtextrem](https://kurtextrem.de/posts/svg-in-js)).

**Is it worth it?** For repeated, scanned structures (sidebars, tab bars, toolbars, file types, status), yes. The byte cost is close to zero and the scanning gain is real. For one-off labels, CTAs and section headings, no. Bytes are not the issue there. The costs are attention, ambiguity and looking templated **[I]**. The owner's portfolio already reflects this. Its only icons are typographic arrows (↗ ↓ ↑ →). Each one inherits the font's weight and colour automatically, which is the web equivalent of SF Symbols' weight matching, and each one means something: leave the site, go down, go back up. The one check needed is that those arrows render from Geist and not from a fallback font with a different weight. If they fall back, swap in a 16px inline SVG at a matching stroke **[R/I]**. The only icon worth adding is the copy/check pair for the email. GitHub and GitLab logos beside text that already says "GitHub" would be the envelope mistake again.

## Specs the taste skill can adopt

### 1. Portfolio skeleton and project-entry spec

| Order | Section | Content rule | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | First screen | Name; role; one-line claim about what you care about; proof by association (current employer, flagship project); one primary CTA (contact) and one secondary (see work). Show the name prominently without letting it fill the desktop screen | [Obs] four admired sites; [M] Ladders headings; [R] owner's "name too big" note |
| 2 | Selected work | **3–5** entries newest or strongest first, with a compact index (no. · title · context · year) linking to them. Merge related projects; move the rest to a "smaller things" list or an archive | [S] NN/g 3–5; [Obs] brittanychiang archive |
| 3 | Experience | Dated list newest first: year range, role, organization, one line, plain-text tech; résumé link | [Obs] brittanychiang |
| 4 | Writing / notes | Only if real; dated list | [Obs] leerob |
| 5 | About / Now | Short and human: what you care about plus one non-work detail. Never a bare skills dump | [O] ADPList red flag; [Obs] paco "Now" |
| 6 | Contact | Visible email with copy-to-clipboard and a "Copied" state; one line of invitation | [Obs] rauno, 21st.dev |
| 7 | Footer | See recipe below | [G] NN/g |

Structural rules: keep this order, which is the category prototype, and put novelty into type, one signature visual, one motion and the artifacts. Never use a card grid of identical tiles for projects. Default to richer proof unless the person is well known. Run the richness check: every section carries content, not just space **[M/R/I]**.

Each project entry carries these fields, in this order: **index number**; **title**; **dates** (month-year range); **context** (client, employer, team size); **one line** saying what it is (at most about 20 words, outcome or problem first); **role** (what *I* decided or built, stated explicitly); **hard part** (one constraint or failure mode); **2–4 decisions**, each a concrete technical choice; **outcome** (a number, users, or shipped state, never invented); **artifact** (the most informative visual: real output, the data shape, the hard screen, or a short muted loop, never a login or marketing screen when something better exists); **stack** (mono, after the outcome); **links** (Live ↗, Code ↗, Write-up →). Use one template for every entry, and vary the artifact rather than the layout **[S]** ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-design-portfolios/)). Containers to choose from, in order of preference for an engineer: sticky-column rows with spec fields; expandable index (`<details>`); typographic list; timeline header. Use a hover-preview index only with touch and keyboard fallbacks, and a playable world only when the medium is the skill **[I]**.

### 2. Footer recipe

| Layer | Contents | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| Sign-off | Name, a one-line motto that echoes the hero claim | Ties the end of the page back to its opening |
| Doormat | Links to sections or projects; on long pages, every project | Visible, labeled, grouped; never collapsed **[G]** |
| Elsewhere | GitHub, LinkedIn or GitLab, CV (PDF), email with copy button | Text labels; ↗ on every link that leaves the site; brand logos only in a repeated row of 5+ |
| Colophon | Type, build, hosting, privacy facts, page weight or third-party request count | Every claim checkable and true; answer questions the page raised |
| Human touch | Local time, availability, last updated, or one easter egg | Pick one or two; don't duplicate what the hero already shows |
| Legal row | © year or "2024–present", back to top | Mono or small caps with tabular figures; same link motion as the rest of the site |
| Hardware | Page-level `<footer>` outside `main` and `section`; 4.5:1 for all text, including labels; 24px targets; footer on the page grid; consistent focus ring | The parts nobody sees, done anyway **[G]** ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/HTML/Element/footer)) |

Size the footer to the page: a short page gets a sign-off line, and a long, rich page gets the full doormat plus colophon, because visitors arrive after a long scroll and use it to navigate **[I]**. Award-site gestures, such as a full-width name or a live clock, sit on top of these layers and never replace contact or navigation **[G/Obs]**.

### 3. Icon decision table, taste rules and performance rules

| Context | Default | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Primary or secondary CTA | Text only; a trailing arrow for "go", or a leading universal glyph (+, download) | [G] NN/g; [I] |
| Destructive action | Text, optionally with an icon; never icon-only outside dense row actions | [G] |
| Mobile tab bar (3–5) | Icon + label, always visible | [G] NN/g; [M] hidden-nav data |
| Desktop sidebar | Icon + label; collapse to icon-only only with tooltips and accessible names | [G/I] |
| Marketing top nav | Text only; never hide primary nav behind a hamburger on desktop | [M] 27% vs 48–50% |
| Dense toolbar, editor, table row actions | Icon-only for universal or established glyphs + tooltip + accessible name; overflow items labeled | [G] NN/g, Soueidan |
| Form fields ("Email" + envelope) | No icon; exceptions: search magnifier, password show/hide | [G/I] |
| Contact or social links | Text; a logo row only when it lists 5+ services; copy action gets a copy→check icon | [I] |
| Feature or section headings | No icon tile; a real visual, number or nothing | [O] slop catalogs |
| Status, file type, inline metadata | Icon + text, never colour alone | [G] WCAG 1.4.1 |
| "AI" features | Never unlabeled sparkles; plain words | [M] NN/g sparkles |
| Emoji as UI icons | Never; only in user content | [I] |

Taste rules: one icon set per product, never mixed. Use the set's native grid (Radix 15, Heroicons mini 20) rather than scaling a 24px glyph down. Size icons to the type (16px beside 13–14px text, 20px beside 15–16px, 24px for touch bars). Match stroke to the label's stem weight: about 1.5 beside regular 14–16px text, heavier beside semibold. Lucide offers an `absoluteStrokeWidth` prop for this; it was not verified in this research. Center icons on the text with `inline-flex; align-items: center; gap: 0.375–0.5em`, and nudge asymmetric glyphs (play, download, chevrons) optically. Colour with `currentColor` or a muted text token; use colour only for state, and always pair it with text. Use round terminals with humanist or geometric sans and sharper terminals with grotesks or mono. That last pairing is opinion with no data **[G/I]** ([Apple HIG](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/icons)). Accessibility: decorative icons get `aria-hidden="true"`. Icon buttons carry their name on the button, preferably as visually hidden text, and any visible label must appear in the accessible name **[G]** ([Sara Soueidan](https://www.sarasoueidan.com/blog/accessible-icon-buttons/); [Lucide](https://lucide.dev/guide/advanced/accessibility)). When the result still looks generic, cut the icon count first, tune stroke and size second, and switch sets last **[I]**.

Performance rules: in React/Next, use named per-icon imports only, never `icons[name]` or a dynamic loader, and add any icon package missing from Next's default optimized list to `optimizePackageImports` **[M]** ([Vercel](https://vercel.com/blog/how-we-optimized-package-imports-in-next-js)). With Phosphor, standardize on one weight and budget about 1 KB per icon **[V]**. For plain HTML, inline SVG for fewer than about ten icons, and a `<symbol>` sprite with `<use>` when the same icons repeat across a page or site **[O]** ([kurtextrem](https://kurtextrem.de/posts/svg-in-js)). In Vite, Astro or Svelte, `unplugin-icons` compiles only the icons used at build time **[V]** ([unplugin-icons](https://github.com/unplugin/unplugin-icons)). Use no icon fonts in new work. If Material Symbols is mandatory, subset with `icon_names`, restrict the axes, and set `display=block` **[M, vendor]** ([Google Fonts](https://developers.google.com/fonts/docs/material_symbols)). A lint can flag four patterns: icon-library imports with a variable name, more than one icon package in `package.json`, an icon element directly above every heading in a repeated group, and icon-only `<button>`s without an accessible name **[I]**.

## Conclusion

The research separates two jobs that portfolio advice usually blends. **Structure is a usability problem with a known answer**: the category prototype, scannable in minutes and routed to proof. **Distinctiveness is a content problem**: the artifacts, the decisions and the details only this person has. A card grid is a symptom of solving neither. It gives every project the same frame because there is nothing specific to put in it. For Leandro, the largest gain is not a new layout. It is writing down his role and outcomes, and *showing the invisible work his hero promises*: the consent table, the questionnaire migration, the real report output.

The same principle covers footers and icons. Care shows where it does work. A colophon is beautiful when its claims are true and checkable, and a footer feels Apple-like when it answers what the page raised and is correct in the accessibility tree that no one sees. Icons are worth adding wherever they speed recognition of repeated items, and nowhere else. For a taste skill, this means rules that subtract by default (no icon tiles, no envelope, no card grid) and add only by evidence (a label, a spec field, a real artifact). Its checks should ask "what does this element prove or speed up?" rather than "does this look designed?"
