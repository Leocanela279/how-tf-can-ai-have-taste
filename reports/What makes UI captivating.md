# Make it familiar, then make it exact

A UI captivates when it is **simple and familiar in structure, exact in its surface, and responsive in its behaviour**, and the evidence for that ordering is stronger than for any individual pixel value. People judge a page's appeal in about 50 ms. The two properties that best predict that verdict are measurable: **low perceived visual complexity**, which means few well-grouped regions rather than little content, and **prototypicality**, which means looking like a typical member of the category. Image metrics plus age and education explain about half the variance in first-impression appeal. Novelty raises preference only while the design stays recognizably typical (Hekkert's "most advanced, yet acceptable", or MAYA). Polish then works as a credibility gate: "design look" was the most-mentioned credibility cue in Stanford's 2,684-person study. This repo's own blind test fits the pattern. The owner preferred a richer, conventional no-skill portfolio over a sparser, structurally novel skill-built one, even though the skill version passed every lint check and the no-skill one failed three. Below that structural layer, the measured core is small: contrast floors (WCAG, APCA), a reading advantage for dark-on-light text that grows as text shrinks, a ~22% time cost for weak button signifiers, a medium preference for curved shapes (g = 0.39), and better tracking of objects when transitions are animated. Almost every concrete number for letter-spacing, line-height, button height, radius, hover delta and easing is **design-system convention**. The conventions agree closely enough to adopt as defaults. That is the right status for them: calibrated defaults for an agent to override with a measured reference or with the owner's stated taste, never laws. The defaults table at the end grades every value by which kind of evidence it rests on.

Evidence tags used throughout:

| Tag | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **[M]** | Measured: a controlled study or usability test with data. |
| **[S]** | Standard: a normative threshold such as WCAG or APCA. These derive from vision science but are not themselves experiments. |
| **[C]** | Convention: what several major design systems (Apple, Material 3, Carbon, Primer, Radix, shadcn, Polaris, Vercel) independently specify. |
| **[O]** | Opinion: one practitioner's craft advice, or a single system's choice. |
| **[R]** | Repo: a first-hand result from this project (n = 1). |
| **[I]** | Inference: this report's own reasoning from the above. |

## Viewers judge in 50 ms, and simple-plus-familiar wins

The "wow" happens before anyone reads a word. Lindgaard and colleagues showed homepages for 500 ms and then for 50 ms, and appeal ratings agreed strongly across both exposures and across repeat viewings. Visual appeal is decided **within 50 ms** and largely sticks **[M]** ([Lindgaard et al. 2006](https://typeset.io/papers/attention-web-designers-you-have-50-milliseconds-to-make-a-3axej7ku89)). A Google team then broke the judgement apart using 119 real-site screenshots. Visual complexity and prototypicality both moved beauty ratings at **17 ms**. Complexity acts first, prototypicality catches up with longer viewing, and **low-complexity, high-prototypicality sites were rated most appealing** **[M]** ([Tuch et al. 2012](https://research.google/pubs/the-role-of-visual-complexity-and-prototypicality-regarding-first-impression-of-websites-working-towards-understanding-aesthetic-judgments/)). Google's own summary: high complexity hurts even a familiar design, and an unfamiliar layout hurts even a simple one ([Google Research blog](https://research.google/blog/users-love-simple-and-familiar-designs-why-websites-need-to-make-a-great-first-impression/)).

The most useful detail for a coding agent is *what* "complexity" means to the eye. Reinecke et al. built models from 450 sites rated at 500 ms. Perceived colorfulness was predicted with **R² = .78** and perceived complexity with **R² = .65**. The strongest single complexity predictor was the **number of leaves in an X-Y cut decomposition** of the layout, i.e. the count of separate visual blocks, together with the number of text groups and image areas. The full appeal model, with demographic interactions, reached **adjusted R² = .48** **[M]** ([Reinecke et al. 2013](https://kgajos.seas.harvard.edu/papers/reinecke13aesthetics.pdf)). The same paper's failure cases matter just as much. Text-heavy pages that were well grouped looked *less* complex than the model predicted. A mostly white page with a few bright buttons looked colourful but not complex. Perception of complexity and colourfulness did not differ between demographic groups; only *preference* did. So complexity is a count of perceptual groups, not of content, and an agent can lower it by aligning and grouping without deleting anything **[I]**. Preferences do shift by audience. Across **2.4 million ratings from about 40,000 people**, women preferred more colourful sites than men, more education went with less appetite for colour, and countries differed in their preferred complexity **[M]** ([Reinecke & Gajos 2014](https://eecs.harvard.edu/~kgajos/papers/2014/reinecke14visual.shtml)). Complexity also has a body cost. In a 48-person study, busier pages raised arousal, lowered valence, increased frown-muscle activity, and slowed visual search and recognition **[M]** ([Tuch et al. 2009](https://edoc.unibas.ch/7990)).

The theory that ties these together is processing fluency: whatever is easy to process feels good. That includes strong figure-ground contrast, symmetry, repetition, clear grouping and prototypicality **[review]** ([Reber, Schwarz & Winkielman 2004](https://pages.ucsd.edu/~pwinkielman/reber-schwarz-winkielman-beauty-PSPR-2004.pdf)). Fluency explains why so many "taste" rules are free: consistent spacing, one radius family and a typical layout are both more beautiful and more usable. The counterweight is Hekkert, Snelders and van Wieringen's MAYA result. Across three product-design studies, typicality and novelty **suppressed each other**: neither predicted preference alone, but each became strongly significant once the other was held constant. **Preference rose with novelty as long as typicality was preserved**, and expertise did not change the pattern **[M]** ([Hekkert et al. 2003](https://research.vu.nl/en/publications/most-advanced-yet-acceptable-typicality-and-novelty-as-joint-pred/)). The practical reading is that novelty belongs in the surface (typeface, one hero visual, one signature motion, a distinctive accent) while the skeleton stays typical (navigation position, page flow, form patterns) **[I]**. Award juries reward the novel edge, and an eye-tracking and heuristics study of Awwwards winners found many of them showed **a significant imbalance between aesthetic innovation and functionality** **[M]** ([Balcerzak & Kwiatkowska, MIDI 2024](https://midi2024.opi.org.pl/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/D1-11-Balancing-Beauty-and-Usability-A-Comprehensive-Evaluation-of-Awwwards-Winning-Websites.pdf)). Award-site "wow" and everyday liking are different targets.

Beauty buys credibility, but only up front. In the Stanford Web Credibility study, **"design look" appeared in 46.1% of comments**, more than any other cue, and in **54.6%** for finance sites **[M, correlational]** ([Consumer Reports / Stanford](https://advocacy.consumerreports.org/research/how-do-people-evaluate-a-web-sites-credibility)). Tractinsky's ATM studies found that beautiful interfaces were rated more usable before and after use ([Tractinsky et al. 2000](https://academic.oup.com/iwc/article/13/2/127/898608)). Yet the best-controlled test reverses it. Tuch et al. crossed aesthetics and usability in an online shop with 80 participants. **Aesthetics did not change perceived usability, but poor usability lowered perceived beauty after use**, with frustration as the likely mediator **[M]** ([Tuch et al. 2012, CHB](https://research.google/pubs/is-beautiful-really-usable-toward-understanding-the-relation-between-usability-aesthetics-and-affect-in-hci/)). Polish wins the first impression on landing pages and onboarding. In a tool people use daily, friction makes the UI look worse over time, so no visual flourish is worth a usability cost there.

Shape preference is real and moderate. A meta-analysis of 61 studies (309 effect sizes) found a reliable preference for curved over angular forms, **Hedges' g = 0.39**. It is not universal: presentation time, stimulus type, expertise and task all moderate it **[M]** ([Chuquichambi et al. 2022](https://research-api.cbs.dk/ws/portalfiles/portal/93945556/erick_g_chuquichambi_et_al_how_universal_is_preference_for_visual_curvaturepublishersversion.pdf)). Context also flips the meaning. Circular logos primed softness and warmth, and angular ones primed durability and hardness **[M]** ([Jiang et al. 2016](https://ira.lib.polyu.edu.hk/handle/10397/64985)). No study isolates button corner radius against clicks, so "rounded is friendlier, sharp is more precise" is an extrapolation **[I]**. Whitespace links to prestige mainly through print-ad research: passive white space raised perceived quality and brand prestige **[M]** ([Pracejus et al., via ASU record](https://asu.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/print-advertising-white-space)). The widely quoted "whitespace improves comprehension by 20%" has no primary source in this research.

What separates the engineer's "wow" from everyone else's is not well studied. Hekkert found expertise did not change the MAYA pattern, and expertise moderates curvature preference, but nothing else measured here compares developers with lay viewers. The best-supported reading is that both groups react to the same fluency signals at first glance **[I]**. Engineers then keep looking, and notice *calibration and behaviour*: headline tracking that tightens with size, ink greys that step down evenly, focus rings that match the button radius, tabular figures that don't jitter, a press response under 100 ms, nothing animating on a keyboard shortcut. The earlier probe of linear.app in this repo is the clearest example. It ships Inter, pills, gradients and borders, all supposed "AI tells", yet reads as expensive. Its headline sits at **56px, line-height 1.1 and −0.022em tracking**, the exact value Inter's own dynamic-metrics formula gives at that size. It uses a 510 weight, and four ink greys at about **18.7, 13.6, 6.1 and 3.5:1** **[R]** ([earlier report](Giving%20AI%20UI%20design%20taste.md)). Lay viewers register these details as "it feels right". Engineers can name them.

## A blind portfolio test picked the richer, more typical page

The repo's first owner-judged experiment pitted two builds of the owner's real portfolio against each other, both built from identical content and screenshots pulled from his GitHub. Variant A was a fresh agent with no skills and a plain prompt. Variant B was built with `skills/taste`, round 1. The deterministic gate scored A worse: **3 errors** (contrast, small targets) and 5 warnings (3 font families, 13 eyebrow labels, 18% monospace text, 12 gradients, blur). B scored **0 errors and 0 warnings**. The owner, judging blind, chose A ("I really really like A"), and asked only for a dark theme **[R]** ([experiments/results.md](../experiments/results.md)).

The literature predicts this result. A read as a recognizable, polished developer portfolio, with a sticky nav, meta strip, live clock, three-column focus strip, project index, a tool card and a dark contact block. B's warm "cinnamon" field and inverted hierarchy were novelty in the *structure*, which is exactly where MAYA says novelty costs typicality. B also removed ornament and filled the gap mostly with space. The taste-skill author's own blind tests had found the same failure: a page that loses its labels, rules and cards "and gained nothing in their place reads as thin" **[R]** ([experiments/results.md](../experiments/results.md)). There is a subtler lesson in the complexity data. Reinecke's complexity is a count of *ungrouped* regions, so A's many parts did not necessarily make it look complex if they were well grouped, and B's austerity did not make it look simpler in any way the viewer rewarded **[I]**. The appeal curve has a floor as well as a ceiling. The research summary "simpler than you think, but not empty" names it, and B went below it.

Two further lessons matter for the skill. First, the gate measures defects and defaults, not liking: it correctly flagged A's contrast and target failures, and those were then fixed without changing the look. Second, the owner's taste *included* several "tier-2 tells": monospace labels, serif italic accents, a warm accent and eyebrows. A tells catalogue is calibration, not law, and should only warn. The dark follow-up shows how a liked design becomes a correct one. The calibrated version uses a warm near-black ground `#0F0E0C` with raised surfaces `#171613`/`#1A1915`, off-white ink `#ECE8DF`, muted `#A39E93`, and an accent lifted to `#EC7044` for dark. Dim labels were raised to 4.5:1 and links padded to 24px targets, and it passes the probe with 0 errors at 1440 and 390 **[R]** ([experiments/results.md](../experiments/results.md)). By my calculation, the ink sits at **15.8:1** on the ground, which is the figure Material cites for its dark theme. The muted grey sits at **7.2:1** and the accent at **6.4:1**. This is one owner and one brief, so it is an anecdote that agrees with the literature, not evidence on its own. The changes it suggests are cheap, though: ask for 2–3 loved sites and light or dark before designing, keep the category's structure, spend novelty on the surface, and add a "does every screen carry enough parts?" richness check to critique.

## Type looks expensive when tracking and leading shrink as size grows

Every system that varies letter-spacing by size draws the same curve. Tracking is slightly positive at 11–12px, zero around 12–13px, negative through body and heading sizes, and most negative at display sizes. Inter publishes this as a formula, `tracking = −0.0223 + 0.185·e^(−0.1745·size)` in em. It works out to **+0.005em at 11px, −0.011em at 16px, −0.017em at 20px, −0.020em at 24px, −0.022em at 32px** and a floor of **−0.0223em from about 40px up** **[C]** ([Inter Dynamic Metrics](https://d.rsms.me/inter-website/v3/dynmetrics/)). Apple's SF table has the same shape at text sizes: +12/1000em at 10pt, 0 at 12pt, −20 at 16pt, −26 at 17pt **[C]** ([Apple HIG Typography](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/typography)). Apple turns *positive* above 24pt, however, because SF's display cut is already tightly spaced. Material 3 sets **+0.5px on 16px body** for Roboto **[C]** ([Material Web tokens](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/material-components/material-web/main/tokens/versions/v0_192/_md-sys-typescale.scss)). Both tables are font-specific, and pasting either onto Inter or Geist makes headlines loose **[I]**. Carbon is conservative: +0.32px at 12px, +0.16px at 14px, zero for most headings, and −0.64px only at display sizes. That restraint is part of why it reads "corporate" rather than "product-tight" **[C]** ([Carbon type sets](https://carbondesignsystem.com/elements/typography/type-sets/)). Tighter brand looks of −0.04 to −0.06em (a reported Geist value) are unverified and should be a deliberate choice, not a default. All-caps labels go the other way. Butterick's rule is to add **5–12% letter-spacing** to caps and small caps, while lowercase body needs none **[O]** ([Practical Typography](https://practicaltypography.com/letterspacing.html)).

Line-height falls as size rises, and the systems specify it as fixed pairs on a 4px grid rather than as a single multiplier. Material runs from **1.12 at display-large (57/64)** through 1.25 for headlines to **1.5 for 16px body** and 1.43 for 14px body. Carbon goes from 1.11 at display to 1.29 for compact component text, 1.43 for 14px body and 1.5 for 16px body **[C]** ([Material Web tokens](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/material-components/material-web/main/tokens/versions/v0_192/_md-sys-typescale.scss); [Carbon](https://carbondesignsystem.com/elements/typography/type-sets/)). Apple's iOS body is tighter at **17/22 (1.29)** because it is tuned for short app lines **[C]** ([Apple HIG](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/typography)). Butterick recommends 120–145% for reading text **[O]** ([Practical Typography](https://practicaltypography.com/line-spacing.html)). Carbon's split between "compact" text inside components (~1.25–1.3) and "reading" text (≥1.43) is a useful rule for an agent. Line length belongs with leading: **45–75 characters, about 66 ideal**, for single-column prose **[O]** ([webtypography.net](http://webtypography.net/2.1.2)). Tailwind's `max-w-prose` (65ch) sits inside that range.

Weight discipline is one of the clearest conventions. Top systems use **three or four weights**: Regular 400 for body, Medium 500 for labels and buttons, Semibold 600 for headings, and Light 300 only at large display sizes, as Carbon does from 42px up **[C]** ([Primer](https://primer.style/product/primitives/typography/); [Carbon](https://carbondesignsystem.com/elements/typography/type-sets/)). Apple tells designers to avoid Ultralight, Thin and Light in UI because they are hard to see at small sizes **[C]** ([Apple HIG](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/typography)). Variable fonts allow calibrated in-between weights, such as Linear's 510 measured in this repo **[R]**. Light text on dark backgrounds looks heavier, especially with macOS subpixel rendering. Robin Rendle lowers variable weight slightly in dark mode (500 → 400 in his body example) and notes that `-webkit-font-smoothing: antialiased` fixes much of the heaviness **[O]** ([CSS-Tricks](https://css-tricks.com/?p=306566)).

For size, app body text sits at **14px** in dense or productive contexts (Carbon productive, M3 body-medium, Geist's "most common" copy-14) and **16–17px** for reading and expressive contexts (Carbon body-02, M3 body-large, iOS 17pt) **[C]** ([Geist typography](https://vercel.com/geist/typography); [Carbon](https://carbondesignsystem.com/elements/typography/type-sets/)). The practical floor is **11–12px**: iOS's minimum is 11pt and M3's smallest role is label-small at 11px **[C]** ([Apple HIG](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/typography)). Mobile web inputs need **≥16px**, because iOS Safari zooms into smaller focused inputs. That rule comes from community sources, not Apple documentation **[O]** ([Rick Strahl](https://weblog.west-wind.com/posts/2023/Apr/17/Preventing-iOS-Textbox-Auto-Zooming-and-ViewPort-Sizing)). No system uses a strict modular ratio. Adjacent M3 and Carbon steps are hand-tuned at about 1.14–1.3, so a hand-picked set (12/13/14/16/20/24/32/40/48/64/72) matches practice better than a pure 1.25 ratio **[I]**. Numbers that align or change in place need `tabular-nums`, and Vercel keeps KPIs and money in the sans, reserving mono for code, paths and IDs **[C]** ([vercel.com/design.md](https://vercel.com/design.md)).

## Buttons earn trust through strong signifiers and states that add contrast

The one rigorous button study is about signifier strength, not size or colour. NN/g tested 71 users on 9 live pages, each with a strong-signifier and a weak (flat) version. Weak signifiers cost **22% more task time and 25% more fixations** (both p < .05). In one pair, 86% of strong-version users went straight to the target versus 50% of weak-version users **[M]** ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/flat-ui-less-attention-cause-uncertainty/)). The pages where flatness did no harm had low density, traditional layout and high-contrast targets, which is the prototypicality story again. Ghost buttons count as weak signifiers. A single CXL homepage test found about **20% fewer clicks** for a ghost variant, which is suggestive, not conclusive **[M, single test]** ([CXL](https://conversionxl.com/blog/ghost-buttons/)). Apple says to prefer buttons with "a discernible background shape and fill" and to keep prominent buttons to one or two per view. Atlassian allows one primary per area **[C]** ([Apple HIG Buttons](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/buttons); [Atlassian](https://atlassian.design/components/button/examples)). The famous "red button +21%" was one small test on a green-dominated page, and it is best read as a contrast and isolation effect. The defensible rule is that the primary CTA is the highest-contrast element in its region, and there is only one **[I]** ([SitePoint](https://www.sitepoint.com/button-ux-red-green/)).

Button dimensions cluster tightly across systems **[C]**. All values below come from source code or official docs.

| System | Default height | Horizontal padding | Label | Radius |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| shadcn/ui | 36px (`h-9`) | 16px (12 with icon) | 14px / 500 | 6px (`rounded-md`) |
| Radix Themes | 32px | 12px | 14px | theme-wide setting |
| Primer | 32px | 12–16px | 14px / 600 | — |
| Polaris | 28px desktop, 32px mobile | 12px | — | 8px |
| Carbon | 40px (48px common) | 16px left, 64px right | 14px / 400 | square |
| Material 3 | 40px | 24px | 14px / 500 | pill |

Sources: [shadcn button.tsx](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/shadcn-ui/ui/main/apps/v4/registry/new-york-v4/ui/button.tsx), [Radix base-button.css](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/radix-ui/themes/main/packages/radix-ui-themes/src/components/_internal/base-button.css), [Primer size tokens](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/primer/primitives/main/src/tokens/functional/size/size.json5), [Polaris Button.module.css](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Shopify/polaris/main/polaris-react/src/components/Button/Button.module.css), [Carbon](https://carbondesignsystem.com/components/button/style/), [M3 tokens](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/material-components/material-web/main/tokens/versions/v0_192/_md-comp-filled-button.scss).

The ratios in the table are more portable than the absolute values **[I]**. Height is **2.3–2.9× the label size**, and below about 2× a button reads as a chip. Horizontal padding is **about 1–1.7em**: dense app UI sits near 1em, expressive and marketing near 1.5em. Icon gaps are 8px at 36–40px heights and 4–6px at 24–32px, and the padding on the icon side drops by about 4px for optical balance (shadcn's `has-[>svg]:px-3`). The working ladder is **32px for dense apps, 36px as a default, 40px comfortable, 44–48px for touch and hero CTAs**. Polaris adds a pattern worth copying: buttons are larger on small screens and smaller from the `md` breakpoint up. Target size has standards behind it. Vercel's guidelines expand any visual target under 24px to a ≥24px hit area and set 44px on mobile, consistent with WCAG 2.5.8's 24×24 CSS px minimum and Apple's **44×44pt** **[S/C]** ([Vercel guidelines](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/vercel-labs/web-interface-guidelines/main/README.md); [W3C 2.5.8](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/target-size-minimum.html); [Apple HIG](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/buttons)).

Shape is a positioning choice, not a quality ranking **[C/I]**. Pills are the current Material 3 and Apple default: Apple says "prefer circular or capsule-shape buttons", with rounded rectangles in vertical stacks. Radii of 6–8px dominate developer and SaaS systems. Square corners are Carbon's deliberate "engineered" signal. Whatever the choice, one radius family should run across buttons, inputs and cards. Nested radii should be **concentric**, with the child radius equal to the parent radius minus the padding. Polaris pairs an 8px button with a 12px focus outline for exactly this reason **[C]** ([Vercel guidelines](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/vercel-labs/web-interface-guidelines/main/README.md); [Polaris](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Shopify/polaris/main/polaris-react/src/components/Button/Button.module.css)).

Spacing follows one convention everywhere: a **4px base with an 8px rhythm (4, 8, 12, 16, 24, 32, 40, 48, 64)** **[C]** ([Radix space tokens](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/radix-ui/themes/main/packages/radix-ui-themes/src/styles/tokens/space.css); [Primer](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/primer/primitives/main/src/tokens/functional/size/size.json5)). The part that reads as taste is the *jump*: gaps inside a group must be clearly smaller than gaps between groups. Linear's spacing histogram packs onto 4/6/8/12 and then jumps to 32, a ratio of about 2.7 **[R]**. That jump is Gestalt proximity made explicit, and it is the cheapest way to lower Reinecke's region count **[I]**. Card padding of 16px (dense), 20–24px (default) and 32px+ (marketing), and section spacing of 64–128px on marketing pages, are common practice that no system document in this research verified **[O]**.

States are where engineers notice craft. Vercel's rule is that **interactions increase contrast**: hover, active and focus all carry more contrast than rest **[C]** ([Vercel guidelines](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/vercel-labs/web-interface-guidelines/main/README.md)). The magnitudes agree across systems:

| State | shadcn | Material 3 | Radix |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hover | `bg-primary/90` | 8% state layer | — |
| Focus | — | 12% state layer | — |
| Press | — | 12% state layer | `brightness(0.92) saturate(1.1)` plus a 1–2px downward label shift |

Sources: [shadcn](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/shadcn-ui/ui/main/apps/v4/registry/new-york-v4/ui/button.tsx); [M3 state tokens](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/material-components/material-web/main/tokens/versions/v0_192/_md-sys-state.scss); [Radix](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/radix-ui/themes/main/packages/radix-ui-themes/src/components/_internal/base-button.css). Press scale of **0.96–0.98** is practitioner convention **[O]**. Hover belongs behind `@media (hover: hover) and (pointer: fine)` so touch devices don't get sticky hover; Tailwind v4's `hover:` already does this.

Focus has a standard. WCAG 2.4.7 (AA) requires a visible indicator. 2.4.13 (AAA) asks for an indicator at least as large as a **2 CSS px perimeter** with **≥3:1 contrast between focused and unfocused pixels**, and a solid 2px outline is the simplest way to comply **[S]** ([W3C Understanding 2.4.13](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/focus-appearance.html)). Use `:focus-visible` so pointer users aren't distracted, and never remove an outline without a replacement. For disabled buttons, shadcn uses `opacity-50`, and Vercel warns against pre-disabling submit buttons, because users then can't trigger validation **[C/O]**. State transitions should be short. The Carbon and M3 micro tokens run **70–150 ms**. Press should be at or below 100 ms so it feels physical. `transition: all` should be replaced by explicit properties (shadcn's own `transition-all` breaks Vercel's rule) **[C/O]** ([Carbon motion](https://carbondesignsystem.com/elements/motion/overview/)).

## Colour comfort comes from restraint, near-extremes and a light default

Contrast has hard floors and a soft ceiling. WCAG 2.2 requires **4.5:1 for text and 3:1 for large text** (about 24px regular or 18.67px bold) and for UI component boundaries. Ratios are not rounded, so 4.499 fails **[S]** ([W3C 1.4.3](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/contrast-minimum.html)). APCA, which is perceptually uniform across light and dark, adds tiers by use:

| APCA level | Use |
|---|---|
| **Lc 90** | Preferred for body text |
| **Lc 75** | Body minimum |
| **Lc 60** | Other content text |
| **Lc 45** | Headlines |
| **Lc 30** | Placeholder and disabled text |
| **Lc 15** | Dividers |

APCA is also the only one of the two to suggest a **maximum of about Lc 90** for large bold text and big colour areas **[S]** ([APCA in a Nutshell](https://git.apcacontrast.com/documentation/APCA_in_a_Nutshell)). Radix builds to this: step 12 text hits Lc 90 and step 11 hits Lc 60, deliberately short of pure black on white (about Lc 106) **[C]** ([Radix scale](https://www.radix-ui.com/colors/docs/palette-composition/understanding-the-scale)). Material's dark theme uses **#121212** rather than #000, and text at **87% / 60% / 38%** white for high, medium and disabled emphasis **[C]** ([Material 2 dark theme](https://m2.material.io/design/color/dark-theme)). Be honest about the status of "avoid pure black and white". No controlled study found here shows harm from #000 on #fff at normal sizes. The case rests on APCA's ceiling, on halation for astigmatic viewers (a plausible mechanism, but untested for polarity), and on vendor convention. It is a strong default, not a prohibition, and true black is legitimate on OLED themes.

Light versus dark is the most commonly mis-stated question in UI. For people with normal vision, **dark text on a light background wins**. Piepenbrock and colleagues found better acuity and proofreading accuracy in light mode for both younger and older adults. The advantage **grows as text gets smaller**, the most likely mechanism is pupil constriction under the brighter display, and participants **did not feel** the difference they performed **[M]** ([NN/g summary](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/dark-mode/); [Mathôt & Ivanov 2019](https://peerj.com/articles/8220)). Polarity made no difference in simulated daytime, but at simulated night light mode was faster for glanceable reading in the MIT AgeLab study. Dark mode helped a small group (7 people) with cloudy ocular media such as cataract **[M]** ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/dark-mode/)). The Cochrane review of 17 RCTs (619 participants) found that blue-light filtering "may not reduce" eye strain, so "easier on the eyes" is not a valid justification for any palette **[M]** ([Cochrane](https://www.cochrane.org/CD013244/EYES_do-blue-light-filtering-spectacle-lenses-reduce-digital-eye-strain)). NN/g's recommendation follows: don't make dark the default for general audiences; offer it and follow the OS setting **[O]**. The repo result adds the counterweight. Preference is not performance, and an owner who wants dark should get dark, done well. That means a warm or tinted near-black ground, elevation by **lighter surfaces rather than shadows**, accents lifted to a **lighter, less saturated tone** (Material's ~200 level) so they don't vibrate, dim text raised to 4.5:1, and small text made larger or heavier, since small text carries the polarity penalty **[C/M/R]**. For a skill, the defaults that follow are: light for reading-heavy and general-audience surfaces; dark when the owner, the category (developer tools, media, portfolios) or the use context (night) calls for it; and always a `prefers-color-scheme` path **[I]**.

Some combinations are hard on the eye for perceptual reasons. Saturated colours far apart on the spectrum, **red against blue above all**, produce chromostereopsis, a depth illusion where one colour seems to float off the other **[M, perceptual]** ([PMC4382974](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4382974/)). Red-green colour vision deficiency affects about **1 in 12 men and 1 in 200 women** ([Live Science](https://www.livescience.com/health/genetics/why-is-color-blindness-so-much-more-common-in-men-than-in-women)). WCAG 1.4.1 forbids colour as the only carrier of meaning **[S]** ([WCAG 2.2](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/#use-of-color)). The rules that follow: separate adjacent strong hues by lightness rather than hue; pair every status colour with an icon or label; prefer blue/orange over red/green for binary encodings; keep full chroma to small areas (buttons, links, badges) and keep large fills neutral or low-chroma. Stripe adds a craft point: WCAG measures contrast, not vibrancy, so a pair can pass and still look muddy **[C]** ([Stripe](https://stripe.com/blog/accessible-color-systems)).

Hue preference has one solid theory. Palmer and Schloss's Ecological Valence Theory explains **about 80% of the variance** in average colour preference from how much people like the objects associated with each colour. Clear blues tend to be liked, and browns and dark yellows, associated with rot and feces, tend to be disliked **[M]** ([Palmer & Schloss 2010](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.0906172107)). In brand research, red reads as **exciting** and blue as **competent**, and saturation and value amplify both effects **[M]** ([Labrecque & Milne 2012](https://econbiz.de/Record/exciting-red-and-competent-blue-the-importance-of-color-in-marketing-labrecque-lauren/10009997374)). Blue is therefore the statistically safest accent and also the least distinctive. Muddy olive and mustard make risky primaries unless an earthy feel is intended. Accent choice should follow personality fit, not a "colour meaning" chart. The "62–90% of judgments are based on colour" figure came from a 2006 literature review that itself stressed the field's inconsistencies, and it is routinely misquoted as "90% of buying decisions". It should never appear in design rationale ([Singh 2006](https://newion.uwinnipeg.ca/~ssingh5/x/color.pdf)).

The palettes of admired products share a structure more than a hue **[C]**. Stripe moved from HSL to CIELAB so that every hue follows one lightness curve, and **any two colours 5+ steps apart pass small-text contrast** (4+ for icons and large text) ([Stripe](https://stripe.com/blog/accessible-color-systems)). Linear generates whole themes in LCH from **3 inputs instead of 98 variables**: base, accent and contrast ([Linear](https://linear.app/now/how-we-redesigned-the-linear-ui)). Radix gives each of its 12 steps a role: backgrounds 1–2, component fills 3–5, borders 6–8, solid accent 9–10, text 11–12 ([Radix](https://www.radix-ui.com/colors/docs/palette-composition/understanding-the-scale)). The result is neutral-dominant, with one accent used for primary actions, selection and focus. Area targets such as "80–90% neutral, accent ≤5–10%" are this report's inference, and the "60-30-10 rule" is interior-design folklore with no UI source **[I]**. Tinting the neutrals slightly toward the brand temperature (OKLCH chroma of about 0.005–0.02) is what makes a palette feel intentional rather than default grey **[C/I]**. It also links to the measured colourfulness finding: accents on a mostly neutral page add perceived colourfulness without adding complexity **[M]** ([Reinecke et al. 2013](https://kgajos.seas.harvard.edu/papers/reinecke13aesthetics.pdf)).

## Motion matters when it explains change, and hurts when it repeats

Transitions matter, but for comprehension and liking, not speed. The best-measured benefit is **object constancy**: knowing a thing is the same thing in a new place or form. In Heer and Robertson's 24-person study, animated transitions beat instant ones for tracking objects in every condition. Staged animation (one change at a time) was preferred over simultaneous animation, which was preferred over static **[M]** ([Heer & Robertson 2007](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~jheer/files/2007-AnimatedTrans-InfoVis.pdf)). Animating viewpoint changes helped users rebuild a spatial map from memory with no time penalty **[M]** ([Bederson & Boltman 1999](https://www.cs.umd.edu/projects/hcil/jazz/learn/papers/CS-TR-3964.pdf)). NN/g's usability observations show the cost of omitting motion: users missed an un-animated cart badge and added items twice, and mistook instant overlays for new pages and pressed Back **[M, qualitative]** ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/animation-purpose-ux/)). Smaller studies find animation raises *liking* without changing task performance. Users often don't consciously notice transitions yet prefer the animated version **[M, small n]** ([Aalto thesis](https://aaltodoc.aalto.fi/items/6c78128d-ceb2-47d5-94d7-9bceae447a9d)). Perceived speed is set by **when new content appears**, not by animation style **[M]** ([Huhtala et al. 2010](https://cris.vtt.fi/en/publications/animated-ui-transitions-and-perception-of-time-a-user-study-on-an/)). Progress bars that **accelerate toward the end** feel faster than linear ones of equal duration **[M, n = 22]** ([Harrison et al. 2007](https://www.chrisharrison.net/projects/progressbars/ProgBarHarrison.pdf)). Skeleton screens are not automatically better. In one practitioner study with about 39 people per group, perceived waits were skeleton **2.82 s**, spinner 2.41 s and blank 2.29 s **[M, small, not peer-reviewed]** ([Viget](https://viget.com/articles/a-bone-to-pick-with-skeleton-screens)).

That evidence yields a three-way rule **[I]**:

| Motion is | When | What to use |
|---|---|---|
| **Required** | A user would miss a change or lose track of where something went: overlays, accordions, reorders, list-to-detail, long waits | Container transform, shared element, layout animation, progress |
| **Optional polish** | The change is clear but abrupt: a toast, popover or tooltip appearing | A fade or small scale under ~200 ms |
| **Decorative** | It carries no information | Low-frequency surfaces only: landing pages, onboarding, empty and success states. Never in tool chrome. |

Material's patterns supply the vocabulary **[C]** ([MDC Motion](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/material-components/material-components-android/master/docs/theming/Motion.md)):

| Pattern | Use |
|---|---|
| Container transform | Card to detail |
| Shared axis | Sequential or hierarchical steps |
| Fade through | Unrelated destinations such as tabs |
| Fade | In-screen enter and exit |

Carbon's split between *productive* motion (efficient, subtle, for tasks) and *expressive* motion (vibrant, for significant moments) maps onto product versus marketing **[C]** ([Carbon](https://carbondesignsystem.com/elements/motion/overview/)). On the web, the View Transitions API now makes shared-element page transitions cheap as progressive enhancement. Same-document transitions ship in **Chrome 111, Safari 18 and Firefox 144**, and cross-document ones in Chrome 126 and Safari 18.2 **[C]** ([Chrome for Developers](https://developer.chrome.com/blog/view-transitions-in-2025)).

Timing is convention, and the conventions agree. NN/g puts simple feedback at about **100 ms**, substantial changes at **200–300 ms**, and calls 400 ms "very slow". Exits should be slightly shorter than entrances, with ease-out for entering and never linear for movement **[C]** ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/animation-duration/)). Carbon's tokens are **70 ms** for buttons and toggles, 110 ms for fades, 150 ms for small expansions, 240 ms for toasts, 400 ms for large expansions and 700 ms for background dimming. Its micro-interactions use ease-out in 90–120 ms **[C]** ([Carbon](https://carbondesignsystem.com/elements/motion/overview/)). Material 3's easing tokens are **standard `cubic-bezier(0.2, 0, 0, 1)`, emphasized-decelerate `(0.05, 0.7, 0.1, 1)` for entering, and emphasized-accelerate `(0.3, 0, 0.8, 0.15)` for exiting**. Its spatial springs use **damping 0.9**, near-critical, so product UI barely bounces **[C]** ([Material tokens](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/material-components/material-web/main/tokens/versions/v0_192/_md-sys-motion.scss); [MDC Motion](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/material-components/material-components-android/master/docs/theming/Motion.md)). The roughly one-second optimum from data-visualization studies applies to chart transitions, not UI chrome.

Overengineering has clear signals. Apple says not to add "motion for the sake of adding motion" and to avoid motion on frequent interactions. It also says no one should have to wait for an animation, especially one they will see more than once **[C]** ([Apple HIG Motion](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/motion.md)). Emil Kowalski goes further: **never animate keyboard-initiated actions**, keep UI motion under 300 ms, and drop animation entirely from tools opened hundreds of times a day. Raycast is his example **[O]** ([Emil Kowalski](https://emilkowal.ski/ui/you-dont-need-animations)). NN/g's tests found several simultaneous animations weakening one another, and motion that only fills downtime frustrating users. In its scrolljacking study, **all participants were disoriented** on a fully scrolljacked page. Parallax on text hurt reading, and users mostly praised effects only when asked **[M, qualitative]** ([NN/g scrolljacking](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolljacking-101/); [NN/g parallax](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/parallax-usability/)). Animation also counts toward perceived complexity, one of four drivers of perceived homepage complexity alongside links, graphics and page length **[M]** ([Geissler et al. via IJSW review](https://journals.shareok.org/ijsw/article/download/67/53)). Accessibility closes the case. WCAG 2.3.3 asks that interaction-triggered motion can be disabled, and it excludes opacity, colour and blur from "motion". So the correct reduced-motion path **swaps movement for a cross-fade** rather than deleting feedback **[S]** ([W3C 2.3.3](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/animation-from-interactions.html)). web.dev recommends making motion opt-in with `@media (prefers-reduced-motion: no-preference)` **[C]** ([web.dev](https://web.dev/articles/prefers-reduced-motion)). Performance rules are simple: animate `transform` and `opacity`, list properties explicitly, and keep animations interruptible **[O, consistent with browser rendering]**. The audit test that falls out of all this: **the product should feel almost the same with motion off**. If removing an animation causes confusion, it was required. If nobody would notice it was gone and it sits on a frequent path, it was overengineering **[I]**.

## Conclusion

The research reframes what a taste skill is for. The measured layer of "captivating" lives above the pixel level: perceived complexity, prototypicality, credibility through polish, and object constancy in motion. Here the right move is almost always conservative. Keep the category's structure, group rather than remove, and animate only to explain. The exact layer (tracking curves, 2.3–2.9× button proportions, 8–10% hover deltas, 70–350 ms timings, Lc 90 ink) is convention. That is good news for an agent: conventions are stable across systems, cheap to encode and easy to lint. They make a UI feel right to engineers, but they don't by themselves make it loved. The B4 result shows the gap between those two layers. A page can be defect-free and still lose to a richer, more typical one with three contrast errors, because the gate measures correctness and the viewer judges fluency and fit.

So the skill should run in two modes that are kept apart. One is a **conservative structural mode**: typical skeleton, enough parts, grouping as the lever for complexity, and the user's own references and theme preference collected first. The other is an **exact surface mode**: the table above as defaults, with novelty concentrated in one or two surface choices. Several questions stay open and are cheap for this repo to test. The literature measures liking, not "wow", and no one has run computational complexity and colourfulness metrics on admired sites against average ones. Whether engineers and lay viewers diverge has never been tested directly. Logging X-Y-cut region counts and Hasler–Süsstrunk colourfulness for every blind-judged build would turn the next round of owner verdicts into data on exactly those gaps.
