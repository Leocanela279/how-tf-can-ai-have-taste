# Mobile attention and uniqueness: what makes a phone UI grab attention, and how to be distinctive without being confusing

Evidence labels on every claim:
- **[measured]**: a primary empirical study or a logged/observational dataset, with the sample size where I found it.
- **[company-measured]**: research reported by the company that ran it (Google, Facebook, Redbooth), not peer reviewed and not independently checked.
- **[consensus]**: platform guidelines (Apple HIG, Material/Android) or repeated expert guidance (NN/g, Baymard) built on their own usability testing.
- **[opinion]**: practitioner, vendor or journalist view.
- **Unverified**: I saw the figure only in a secondary source or a search snippet.

## 1. How fast are first impressions formed on mobile, and what drives them?

### Takeaway
The one mobile-specific study I found (Miniukovich & De Angeli 2014) reports that mobile app first impressions, like desktop ones, **form fast and persist**. In that study, simple image metrics explain about **40% of perceived complexity and 36% of perceived aesthetics**, close to the desktop figures. There is no mobile replication of Tuch et al.'s 17–50 ms complexity × prototypicality result, so "low complexity + high prototypicality" on phones is an **extrapolation, but a consistent one**. The largest and newest mobile dataset is Google's Material 3 Expressive research (46 studies, 18,000+ people). Its finding is that **expressive emphasis aimed at the key element** (size, shape, colour, containment) made people find it **up to 4× faster**. That is novelty in service of hierarchy, not decoration. The evidence is company-reported.

### Cited Findings
- **[measured] Miniukovich & De Angeli, "Visual Impressions of Mobile App Interfaces", NordiCHI '14, pp. 31–40, DOI 10.1145/2639189.2641219.** Two studies. Study 1 "confirmed the persistence of first impressions on mobile interfaces evaluation." Their automatic image metrics explained **40% of variance in subjective complexity and 36% of variance in aesthetics**. Exposure times and N were not visible in the extracts I could reach. — [ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/283453332_Visual_Impressions_of_Mobile_App_Interfaces); [Academia.edu copy](https://www.academia.edu/11396920/Visual_Impressions_of_Mobile_App_Interfaces); [dblp](https://dblp.org/pid/138/0855.html)
- **[opinion/theory within academic papers]** Researchers argue that smartphone users "might value simplicity even more" because on-the-go use brings outside distractions and "short and intensive interaction periods". — summarised in search extracts around [Facets of simplicity for the smartphone interface (IJHCS)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1071581911001261). I could not check exactly which paper says this.
- **[measured]** An eye-tracking study of mobile news apps split visual complexity into **quantity complexity** (how many elements) and **layout complexity** (how they are arranged), and tested their effects on search behaviour and satisfaction. Effect sizes were not visible in the extract. — [Universal Access in the Information Society, 2021](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1007/s10209-021-00815-1)
- **[measured]** One app-aesthetics paper notes that "while there are several studies that show that aesthetics are at the core of first impressions in website design, no such concrete evidence is available for mobile apps." The authors say this gap motivated their study. — [Understanding Visual Appeal and Quality Perceptions of Mobile Apps (Springer, HCII 2015)](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-319-20901-2_42)
- **[measured] Desktop baseline (for comparison only):** Tuch et al. (2012, Google) found that low visual complexity and high prototypicality predicted appeal at 17–50 ms. — [Google Research PDF](https://research.google.com/pubs/archive/38315.pdf); [Google blog](https://research.google/blog/users-love-simple-and-familiar-designs-why-websites-need-to-make-a-great-first-impression/)
- **[company-measured] Material 3 Expressive (Google, May 2025):** 46 research studies, "hundreds of designs", **18,000+ participants** worldwide. In eye-tracking, users spotted key UI elements **up to 4× faster** with the expressive designs. The example given was the email Send button. **Older participants found key elements as fast as younger ones**, which narrowed an age gap. **87% of 18–24-year-olds** preferred the expressive designs. Google itself cautioned that emotion-centred design "is not a solution for all situations." — [Dezeen](https://www.dezeen.com/2025/05/28/google-ushers-in-age-of-expressive-interfaces-with-material-design-update/amp/); [9to5Google (on the accidentally published blog post)](https://9to5google.com/?p=671309); [Fast Company](https://www.fastcompany.com/91333562/google-maximalist-new-material-design-kit). The original design.google article could not be fetched.
- **[measured] Webpage aesthetics, general:** a separate study (N = 194) found the highest ratings went to high symmetry, low complexity, blue hue, medium brightness and medium-to-high saturation. It is not mobile-specific. — surfaced in the same search; source page: [Webpage Aesthetics (ACM NordiCHI 2016)](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/2971485.2971544). **Unverified** that this paper is the N = 194 study.

### Inferences
- On mobile, the desktop rule probably holds and may hold more strongly, since a 390 px column leaves little room for complexity to hide. The mobile evidence agrees on persistence and on metric-predictable aesthetics, but nobody has re-run the prototypicality manipulation on phones.
- Material 3 Expressive refines MAYA for mobile. "Expressive" did not mean new structure. It meant **stronger emphasis on the one element that matters** in a conventional layout (bigger, differently shaped, more saturated Send button). Distinctiveness that **sharpens hierarchy** speeds people up. Distinctiveness that **adds elements** raises complexity, which hurts first impressions.
- Rule for AI: one dominant element per screen, made distinctive by size, shape and colour. Everything else stays typical and quiet.

### Gaps
- No mobile replication of Tuch 2012's 17/50 ms complexity × prototypicality design was found. Exposure times and N for Miniukovich & De Angeli 2014 could not be checked, because the full text was unreachable.
- "Smartphone app aesthetics influence users' experience and performance" (2023) came up in search. Its findings were not visible. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/371591890_Smartphone_app_aesthetics_influence_users'_experience_and_performance)
- No mobile-specific studies were found on typeface choice or colour at first impression.

## 2. What must the first viewport (~390×844) contain? Hero patterns that work vs fail

### Takeaway
There is no phone-specific eye-tracking number for "above the fold". The desktop data (NN/g 2018) shows the first screen still gets the largest share of attention, at **57%**. The evidence is clearest about what fails. **Auto-rotating carousels** are a consistent problem; Baymard says that on mobile they "shouldn't autorotate at all" and found **46%** of carousel implementations have usability problems. **Autoplaying video heroes** are unexpected and unwelcome, cost data, may not play at all, and harm vestibular and photosensitive users. **Scrolljacked or parallax heroes** disorient people. What works is a viewport that says what the thing is, shows one real visual, and gives one obvious next action, with content visibly continuing below. That last part is expert consensus and inference, not a measured mobile result.

### Cited Findings
- **[measured, desktop] NN/g 2018 (120 participants, 1920×1080):** the first screenful got **57%** of viewing time, down from 80% in 2010. The second got **17%**, then 7%, 5%, 3%, 2%. The first two screens together got **74%**. — [NN/g, Scrolling and Attention](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/). No phone-equivalent figure was found.
- **[consensus] NN/g homepage principles:** visitors "typically do not expect, nor appreciate, being immediately greeted with loud, moving content". Under WCAG, auto-playing motion that lasts more than 5 seconds needs a pause/stop control. — [NN/g, Homepage Design: 5 Fundamental Principles](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/homepage-design-principles/)
- **[consensus] Baymard:** 46% of desktop and mobile sites with a homepage carousel implement it with usability issues. Baymard's 2026 benchmark separately reports that 31% of carousel sites "implement them incorrectly"; these are different pages and scopes. "On mobile sites, carousel slides shouldn't autorotate at all (due to the lack of hover)." Most users never see all the slides. Controls must be easy to tap and support swipe. An alternative, "static content sections scattered throughout the homepage in combination with featured categories", "performs as well with users and is technically much simpler." — [Baymard, 10 UX Requirements for Homepage Carousels](https://baymard.com/blog/homepage-carousel); [Baymard on X, 46%](https://x.com/Baymard/status/1907789888671359463); [Baymard 2026 navigation benchmark](https://baymard.com/research-articles/ecommerce-navigation-best-practice)
- **[measured, benchmark] Baymard:** 65% of mobile sites' homepage and category navigation is "mediocre" to "poor". This comes from 17,000+ UX performance scores across 180+ top US/EU e-commerce sites. — [Baymard, mobile search & category navigation](https://baymard.com/research-articles/mobile-ecommerce-search-and-navigation)
- **[consensus, secondary] NN/g mobile guidance, as quoted by others:** do not auto-forward carousels on phones. People scroll quickly past short heroes, so the slide changes below the fold where nobody sees it. — [UMBC FAQ quoting NN/g](https://umbc.atlassian.net/wiki/x/1GTVAQ); [Texas A&M web guidance](https://web.tamu.edu/?p=1845). **Secondary.**
- **[consensus] Harvard design system (Feb 2025):** recommends **prohibiting autoplaying hero videos**, because they "can be disruptive and even harmful" to people with photosensitive epilepsy or vestibular disorders. — [HarvardSites Design System](https://designsystem.harvardsites.harvard.edu/news/2025/02/autoplaying-hero-background-videos-digital-design)
- **[opinion]** Autoplay video on mobile uses extra data, and some phones won't play it. On iOS, video must be muted, looping and `playsinline`, or it is forced fullscreen. Recommended mobile fallback: a static image. — [Simon Hearne, Fast and Responsive Hero Videos](https://simonhearne.com/2021/fast-responsive-videos/); [ignite.video](https://www.ignite.video/en/articles/basics/autoplay-videos); [designtlc](https://designtlc.com/how-to-optimize-a-silent-background-video-for-your-websites-hero-area/)
- **[opinion]** Type-first heroes are described as mobile-friendly because text scales better than images. A trend roundup reports that **41% of award homepages show ≥80 px text in the first screen, versus 19% of SaaS homepages**. That is a single blog's sample and **unverified**; the most likely source is the "We scanned 137 sites" piece. — [Webyansh trends](https://webyansh.com/post/top-web-design-trends); [Malbar Design, typography as hero](https://malbardesign.com/typography-as-hero-image-trend/)

### Inferences
- The "huge empty hero" fails harder on phones than on desktop. A 100vh hero with a centred headline and a gradient uses the most-viewed screen on zero information. On 390×844, browser chrome and the safe area make the visible area smaller still, so the hero should be **shorter than the viewport** and let the next section peek in. That gives a scroll cue without needing an arrow.
- Rules for AI, first mobile viewport:
  1. State what this is, in plain words, in ≤ 2 lines at a large size. The headline itself can be the visual.
  2. Show **one** real visual (product, photo or data), cropped tight. No abstract gradient blob.
  3. Show one primary action within thumb reach. Optionally add one secondary text link.
  4. Let the top of the next section show (roughly the last 10–15% of the screen) so continuation is visible.
  5. No auto-rotation, no autoplay video with motion over 5 s, no scroll hijacking in the hero. Swap video for a poster image on phones and under `prefers-reduced-motion`.

### Gaps
- No phone-specific fold or attention-distribution study was found. NN/g's 2018 data is desktop only.
- No measured A/B comparison was found of type-only heroes versus image heroes on mobile.

## 3. How do award-level mobile sites and acclaimed apps get distinctiveness?

### Takeaway
Across the examples I could check (Airbnb 2025, Duolingo, Family, Apple's motion and haptics guidance, Rauno Freiberg and Emil Kowalski's craft writing), distinctiveness sits in a **small set of surface carriers**: a signature visual asset (Airbnb's animated 3D "Lava" icons, Duolingo's characters), **motion that responds to state and to the finger** (interruptible springs that keep gesture momentum), and **haptics in sync with visuals**. The navigation skeleton stays conventional: tab bar, standard back behaviour, sheets. Awwwards' own mobile judging is usability-first: a Mobile Excellence badge needs **≥75/100** on Google-derived usability, friendliness and performance checks, and creativity is only one of four criteria for Mobile Site of the Year.

### Cited Findings
- **[consensus] Awwwards Mobile Excellence:** every submission is scored, and sites at **75/100 or above** get the badge. The checklist is "based on Google's criteria", covering usability, friendliness and performance: viewport, tap target size, font legibility, contrast. Mobile Site of the Year is judged on **Usability, Creativity, Content, Design**. — [Awwwards × Google announcement](https://www.awwwards.com/google-and-awwwards-present-the-mobile-excellence-award.html); [Mobile Excellence guidelines PDF](https://www.awwwards.com/mobile-excellence-guidelines.pdf). A 2026 blog says 70/100, which conflicts with the official figure: [hontran.dev](https://www.hontran.dev/blog/awwwards-judging-criteria).
- **[consensus] Greenspector** analysed the 10 Awwwards mobile-excellence nominees for footprint and performance. Details were not reachable. — [Greenspector](https://greenspector.com/en/analysis_sites_nominated_mobile_excellence_awwwards/)
- **[opinion]** Most of the louder award-site trends (custom cursors, WebGL heroes) "stay on award sites and rarely reach business websites". — [Figma, web design trends 2026](https://www.figma.com/resource-library/web-design-trends/) (attribution from search extract)
- **[opinion/press] Airbnb, May 2025 redesign:** Airbnb describes "a dimensional and beautifully animated interface – including with new 3D iconography". Bloomberg: tap the house icon "and its teeny front door pops open and porch light illuminates." The icon format ("Lava") is reportedly a custom lightweight animated format, but Airbnb has not documented it publicly. — [Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-06-13/apple-airbnb-ditch-flat-app-icons-for-new-3d-ui-design); [Techbuzz Ireland (release text)](https://techbuzzireland.com/2025/05/14/redesigned-airbnb-app-now-available/); [Medium technical speculation](https://medium.com/@waldobear002/airbnbs-new-lava-icon-format-a-technical-deep-dive-b2604626c7e0)
- **[opinion/vendor] Duolingo:** characters run on Rive state machines with layered states (idle breathing, blinking, eye darts, correct/wrong reactions). Lip sync uses 10–20+ mouth shapes. Rive says 8 head × 8 body animations produce 64+ variations in a file under 1 MB. All of this is vendor or secondary material. — [Rive blog, Duolingo Lily](https://rive.app/blog/duolingo-s-ai-powered-video-call-brings-lily-to-life); [DEV community write-up](https://dev.to/uianimation/how-duolingo-uses-rive-for-their-character-animation-and-how-you-can-build-a-similar-rive-mascot-5d19)
- **[opinion] Family wallet (Benji Taylor / Los Feliz Engineering):** at launch it was described as a "simple interface and clear flows make it delightful and fun to use". Craft details cited include a QR-code "sequin" easter egg, confetti on backup completion, and a widely copied "dynamic tray" system of sheets that expand, contract and adapt. — [Family launch post](https://family.co/blog/launch); [yespress profile](https://yespress.io/benji-taylor) (fan-style profile, low reliability)
- **[opinion, expert craft] Rauno Freiberg, "Invisible Details of Interaction Design" (2023):** good interactions borrow physical properties. **Interruptibility**: "a book page can be turned mid-motion". **Momentum**: an app dismissing into the Dynamic Island keeps "the momentum and angle of the throw". **Trigger timing**: a swipe action should fire when elements reach their final position, not only on finger lift, because waiting for release feels sluggish. — [rauno.me](https://rauno.me/craft/interaction-design); [Every reprint](https://every.to/p/invisible-details-of-interaction-design)
- **[opinion, expert craft] Emil Kowalski, frequency rule:** animate in inverse proportion to how often something happens. Actions done 100+ times a day (keyboard shortcuts, command palette) get **no** animation. Occasional ones (modals, toasts) get standard animation. Rare moments (onboarding, completion) may carry delight. Default to ease-out for enter/exit and avoid ease-in. Keep UI motion under ~300 ms. The exact thresholds are the skill's distillation. — [emilkowalski/skills STANDARDS.md (GitHub)](https://github.com/emilkowalski/skills/blob/main/skills/review-animations/STANDARDS.md)
- **[consensus] Apple HIG, Playing haptics:** "Use system-provided haptic patterns according to their documented meanings." "Prefer using haptics to complement other feedback… match the intensity and sharpness of a haptic with the intensity and sharpness of the animation it accompanies." "In most apps, prefer playing short haptics that complement discrete events… long-running haptics in an app can dilute the meaning." — [Apple HIG, Playing haptics](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/playing-haptics)
- **[consensus] Apple HIG, Layout:** "Familiar relationships between controls and content let people use and discover features right away". It also says to extend full-screen background content under toolbars and tab bars, and to differentiate controls from content. — [Apple HIG, Layout](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/layout)

### Inferences
- Award-level mobile distinctiveness is **one ownable asset plus physical-feeling interaction**, not new layout. The asset can be a type treatment, an illustration or icon family, or a character. Airbnb kept its tab bar and search-first structure and made the icons the signature. Duolingo kept a conventional lesson flow and made the character react to state.
- On mobile, "motion and haptics as one gesture" is the craft signal the web rarely has. On the mobile web, that means springs that follow the finger, interruptible transitions, and the Vibration API where supported. iOS Safari does not support the Vibration API, so do not depend on it.
- Rules for AI:
  1. Pick **one** signature carrier per product (display type, illustration or icon family, or one hero motion) and repeat it.
  2. Tie motion to **state change or the finger**, never to page load alone.
  3. Rare moments (success, completion, first run) may celebrate. Frequent actions get instant feedback with no choreography.

### Gaps
- No Linear-mobile, Arc-mobile, Things or Revolut design-blog sources were reached in this session. Their distinctiveness techniques are not documented here.
- No mobile-specific Godly or Awwwards gallery analysis was found that measures how often award sites use full-bleed imagery, oversized type or scroll storytelling on phones.
- Duolingo's use of haptics: no source found.

## 4. Uniqueness vs intuitiveness: where can you break convention on mobile, and where never?

### Takeaway
The evidence is strongest for keeping **navigation visible and conventional**. NN/g (179 participants) found hidden navigation less discoverable and slower, though the penalty is smaller on mobile than desktop. Companies that moved from a hamburger to a bottom tab bar (Facebook, Redbooth) report engagement gains; the figures are company-reported. Apple and Android both say 3–5 visible tab or navigation destinations. **Gestures are the least discoverable layer.** NN/g and Apple both say custom gestures must be shortcuts that sit on top of visible controls, never replacements for them. Thumb ergonomics (Hoober: 49% one-handed, ~75% thumb-driven; touch accuracy 7 mm at centre to 12 mm at corners) argue for primary actions low and central. NN/g's 2025 critique of iOS 26 Liquid Glass shows that even Apple pays a usability cost when novelty hits control legibility and moves conventions such as search to the bottom.

### Cited Findings
- **[measured] NN/g hidden-navigation study (2016, N = 179, desktop and mobile):** hidden navigation was less discoverable than visible or combo navigation, and was worse for task difficulty, time and success. "The impact on mobile was not as big as on the desktop" and varied by site. Causes: the extra step to expose options, and small menu icons near other targets. — [NN/g, Beyond the Hamburger: What Makes Navigation Discoverable on Mobile](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/find-navigation-mobile-even-hamburger/); [NN/g, Hamburger Menus and Hidden Navigation](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/hamburger-menus/); [methodology](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/hidden-navigation-methodology/)
  - Secondary figures: a ~20% discoverability drop for hidden nav, 39% slower on desktop and **15% slower on mobile**. **Unverified.** — [Smart Insights summary](https://www.smartinsights.com/digital-marketing-strategy/are-hamburger-menus-hidden-navigation-good-for-ux/)
  - Later NN/g work: the hamburger icon is now widely recognised, especially in its standard spot and style, but the advice to make navigation visible where possible stands. — [NN/g, hamburger icon recognizability](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/hamburger-menu-icon-recognizability/)
- **[company-measured, via LukeW] Hamburger → tab bar:** Facebook's iOS app saw engagement, satisfaction, revenue and perceived-speed metrics improve after moving to a bottom tab bar. Redbooth saw increased sessions and users; secondary sources claim +65% DAU and +70% session time, **unverified**. Counter-cases: Polar's engagement "plummeted" after moving *to* a toggle menu, and Zeebox's dropped after moving to a drawer. A 2014 TechCrunch-reported A/B test credited the tab bar with 55% higher weekly use frequency; **unverified**, and the company is unclear. — [LukeW, Obvious Always Wins / tab bar entry](https://www.lukew.com/ff/entry.asp?1945=); [Smashing Magazine, Basic Patterns for Mobile Navigation](https://www.smashingmagazine.com/2017/05/basic-patterns-mobile-navigation/); [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/?p=1007141)
- **[consensus] Apple HIG, Tab bars (current, iOS 26):** "Use a tab bar to support navigation, not to provide actions." "Make sure the tab bar is visible when people navigate to different sections… If you hide the tab bar, people can forget which area of the app they're in." "Avoid overflow tabs." "Don't disable or hide tab bar buttons." "Include tab labels… Use single words whenever possible." The tab bar "floats above content at the bottom of the screen" on Liquid Glass and can minimise on scroll when it has an accessory. iPad guidance: "aim for a default list of five or fewer." — [Apple HIG, Tab bars](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/tab-bars)
- **[consensus] Android / Material 3:** use a navigation bar for "three to five destinations of equal importance" at compact sizes. A navigation drawer can hold more than five but "is not as ideal as the navigation bar… due to the need to reach to the top bar on compact sizes." — [Android Developers, Navigation bar (Compose)](https://developer.android.com/develop/ui/compose/components/navigation-bar); [Android layout & nav patterns](https://developer.android.com/design/ui/mobile/guides/layout-and-content/layout-and-nav-patterns?hl=en)
- **[consensus] Apple HIG, Gestures:** "Avoid using a familiar gesture like tap or swipe to perform an action that's unique to your app; similarly, avoid creating a unique gesture to perform a standard action." Custom gestures should be "Discoverable, Straightforward to perform, Distinct from other gestures, Not the only way to perform an important action." "Use shortcut gestures to supplement standard gestures, not replace them" (example: keep the Back button even with edge-swipe). "Avoid conflicting with gestures that access system UI." "Indicate when a gesture isn't available." — [Apple HIG, Gestures](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/gestures)
- **[consensus] NN/g on gestures:** contextual-menu gestures "are not discoverable and have still not become standard", so "include other ways to perform the actions, that don't rely on gestures." One-time onboarding tips fail because people are "unlikely… to remember to use it later." Swipes need "a visible cue… or they might never do so." Avoid "swipe ambiguity", where the same swipe means different things in different parts of one screen. NN/g's example: Sephora's menu, reachable only by a horizontal edge swipe, collides with iOS back. — [NN/g, Contextual Swipe](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/contextual-swipe/); [NN/g, Contextual Menus](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/contextual-menus/); [NN/g, iPhone X: The Rise of Gestures](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/iphone-x/); [NN/g, Mobile Usability 2nd Study](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mobile-usability-2nd-study/)
- **[consensus] Apple HIG, Sheets:** sheets suit "a simple task that they can complete before returning to the parent view". "For complex or prolonged user flows, consider alternatives to sheets." "Display only one sheet at a time." Detents: system **medium (≈ half) and large**, plus custom heights. Always pair Done with Cancel or Back. — [Apple HIG, Sheets](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/sheets)
- **[measured, observational] Hoober (2013), 1,333 observations, 780 touching the screen:** **49%** one-handed, **36%** cradled, **15%** two-handed; thumbs drive about **75%** of interactions. Hoober says the study is "not scientific", and it excluded tablets. — [UXmatters, How Do Users Really Hold Mobile Devices?](https://www.uxmatters.com/mt/archives/2013/02/how-do-users-really-hold-mobile-devices.php)
- **[measured, large log + observation] Hoober (2017):** touch accuracy needed for 95% of taps is about **7 mm at the centre, 9 mm along edges, 12 mm in corners**. Smashing quotes 11 mm top / 12 mm bottom. Data includes "several million" taps from an Android game, with a cited dataset of 120M+ touch events. People tend to look at and tap the **centre** first. — [UXmatters, Design for Fingers, Touch and People, Part 3](https://www.uxmatters.com/mt/archives/2017/07/design-for-fingers-touch-and-people-part-3.php); [4ourth Mobile, Designing for Touch](https://www.4ourthmobile.com/publications/designing-for-touch); [Smashing, Accessible Target Sizes Cheatsheet](https://www.smashingmagazine.com/2023/04/accessible-tap-target-sizes-rage-taps-clicks/)
- **[measured] Bergstrom-Lehtovirta & Oulasvirta, CHI 2014:** a model of the thumb's functional area predicted from surface size, hand size and index-finger position. "Model fit is high" for 20 participants. Designers can use it to check whether a layout suits thumb use. — [CHI 2014 record](https://researchr.org/publication/Bergstrom-LehtovirtaO14); [KU publication page](https://test01.ku.dk/:obvius/pureproxy/3974268/en/publications/modeling-the-functional-area-of-the-thumb-on-mobile-touchscreen-s)
- **[measured] Avrahami (FXPAL, CHI 2015):** error rates did **not** differ by distance from the screen edge, but small targets had significantly more errors than larger ones. This supports size over position. — [The Effect of Edge Targets on Touch Performance (ACM)](https://dl.acm.org/doi/pdf/10.1145/2702123.2702439?download=true)
- **[consensus] Target sizes:** Apple **44 pt** (~7 mm); Material **48 dp** (~9 mm). — [Smashing, Fitts' Law in the Touch Era](https://www.smashingmagazine.com/2022/02/fitts-law-touch-era/)
- **[consensus / expert heuristic] NN/g, "Liquid Glass Is Cracked, and Usability Suffers in iOS 26" (2025):** the visual language "obscures content instead of letting it take the spotlight". Layered translucency hurts legibility. Tab bars are cramped, with shrunken targets. Motion is "distraction with a side of nausea". Moving search to the bottom breaks learned habits. This is a heuristic review, not measured testing. After the backlash, Apple added a "Tinted" option in iOS 26.1 betas. — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/liquid-glass/); [MacRumors](https://www.macrumors.com/2025/09/17/ios-26-liquid-glass-critiques/); [Gulf News on iOS 26.1 Tinted](https://gulfnews.com/technology/companies/apple-yields-tinted-control-in-ios-261-beta-4-tones-down-liquid-glass-after-backlash-1.500315176)

### Inferences
- **The MAYA split holds on mobile, and the safe and unsafe zones can be named:**
  - **Never break** (structure): visible primary navigation (a 3–5 item bottom tab bar with labels in apps; a visible top-level link set or bottom bar on the mobile web); a Back control and system back/edge-swipe; vertical scroll at native speed; tap = activate; system gestures (home indicator, edge swipe, pull-to-refresh where expected); 44–48 pt targets; text contrast and legibility; one sheet at a time.
  - **Bend with care** (behaviour): sheets with custom detents; swipe actions that **also** exist as visible buttons; horizontal swipe galleries that **show a peeking next card** as the cue; minimising bars on scroll.
  - **Free to own** (surface): display typography, colour, illustration and iconography, imagery crops, the motion personality of transitions (springiness, choreography), haptic accents, empty and success states, copy voice.
- Apple's own Liquid Glass reception is a warning case for a taste skill. Novelty applied to the **control layer** (translucent, reflowing, relocated controls) costs legibility and learned habit, even from the platform owner. Put novelty in content and branding, not in the chrome people navigate with.
- Thumb data gives a layout rule as well as a constraint: the primary action sits low-centre. The top corners suit rare or destructive actions. Hoober's centre-first finding means the most important *content* also belongs mid-screen, not crammed under the status bar.

### Gaps
- Exact NN/g mobile discoverability percentages for hidden vs combo navigation could not be verified because the site was unreachable. A commonly recalled figure is "57% vs 86% usage on mobile", but I could not confirm it in this session, so do not cite it without checking.
- No 2020–2026 controlled study was found comparing hamburger and tab bar on the **mobile web** specifically. The company data is app-based and about 10 years old.
- Material 3 bottom-sheet guidance could not be retrieved, because m3.material.io was unreachable.

## 5. Motion as attention on mobile: what helps, what hurts, and reduced motion

### Takeaway
Motion helps when it is **feedback, continuity or a direct response to the finger**: brief, precise, interruptible, spring-based, and in sync with haptics. Apple's HIG says this almost word for word, and SwiftUI made springs (defined by duration and bounce, preserving velocity on interruption) the default animation. Motion hurts when it is **imposed**. Scrolljacking disorients most participants in NN/g testing. Parallax is tuned out as "visual noise" and slows loading. Autoplay and peripheral oscillation cause discomfort. The population at risk is not small: **35.4% of US adults aged 40+** showed vestibular dysfunction in national survey data.

### Cited Findings
- **[consensus] Apple HIG, Motion (updated Sept 2025 for Liquid Glass):** "Add motion purposefully… Don't add motion for the sake of adding motion. Gratuitous or excessive animation can distract people and may make them feel disconnected or physically uncomfortable." "Make motion optional… avoid using it as the only way to communicate important information." "Strive for realistic feedback motion that follows people's gestures… if someone reveals a view by sliding it down from the top, they don't expect to dismiss the view by sliding it to the side." "Aim for brevity and precision in feedback animations." "Generally avoid adding motion to UI interactions that occur frequently." "Let people cancel motion… don't make people wait for an animation to complete." System motion "responds to direct touch interaction with greater emphasis… but produces a more subdued effect" with a trackpad. visionOS adds: avoid sustained oscillation near **0.2 Hz**. — [Apple HIG, Motion](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/motion)
- **[consensus] Apple WWDC23, "Animate with springs" (session 10158):** springs are specified by **duration and bounce**, with presets **smooth, snappy, bouncy**. When a spring is retargeted mid-flight it "carries over its current velocity", which keeps interruptions natural. SwiftUI tracks gesture velocity automatically. **Springs are now SwiftUI's default animation.** Don't wait for full settling on user-facing changes; use the perceptual duration. — [Apple Developer video](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2023/10158/); [WWDC Notes](https://wwdcnotes.com/notes/wwdc23/10158)
- **[measured, usability testing] NN/g, Scrolljacking 101:** sites with scrolljacking showed "significant threats to user control and freedom, discoverability, attention, efficiency, and task success". "The majority of our study participants were at least mildly disoriented". Some left or refreshed. Scroll-fading inside scrolljacked sections overwhelmed people. N was not visible in the extract. — [NN/g, Scrolljacking 101](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolljacking-101/)
- **[consensus] NN/g, What Parallax Lacks:** even subtle modern parallax causes slow loading and hard-to-read content. It "barely accomplish[es] their intended effect of holding our attention" because users have learned to treat it as visual noise, which NN/g likens to banner blindness. — [NN/g, What Parallax Lacks](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/parallax-usability/); [NN/g, Animation duration](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/animation-duration/)
- **[measured, epidemiological] Agrawal et al., NHANES 2001–2004:** **35.4%** of US adults aged 40+ (about 69 million people) had vestibular dysfunction by an objective balance test. Prevalence rises from 18.5% (40s) to 85% (80+). As many as 32% of people over 40 without dizziness symptoms showed it. — [JAMA Internal Medicine](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/773517); [Frontiers in Neurology 2016](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/neurology/articles/10.3389/fneur.2016.00231/full)
- **[measured, survey; secondary] WebAIM** is reported to have found that over 50% of respondents with vestibular impairments named parallax scrolling as a trigger. **Unverified secondary.** — [Greenlit Content](https://greenlitcontent.com/marketing/scrolljacking-the-good-the-bad-and-the-ugly)
- **[opinion, expert craft]** Interruptibility and momentum preservation (Freiberg) and the frequency rule (Kowalski) are cited in section 3. — [rauno.me](https://rauno.me/craft/interaction-design); [emilkowalski/skills](https://github.com/emilkowalski/skills/blob/main/skills/review-animations/STANDARDS.md)

### Inferences
- **Motion attracts attention only when it is rare and meaningful.** Ambient motion (parallax, looping video, floating blobs) gets filtered out as noise, so it costs performance and comfort and buys no attention. Motion on a *state change the user caused* gets noticed and is welcome.
- Rules for AI, mobile motion:
  1. Every animation answers "what changed, and where did it go?" (feedback or continuity), or it is cut.
  2. Use springs (or spring-like ease-out curves on the web), interruptible, carrying gesture velocity. Enter/exit under ~300 ms. No ease-in on user-triggered UI.
  3. Never take over scroll pace or direction. No parallax on body content. No autoplay loops longer than 5 s without a pause control.
  4. Under `prefers-reduced-motion`: replace movement with opacity or crossfade, and keep state feedback. Ship this as the tested path, not an afterthought.
  5. Pair one haptic with one discrete event where the platform supports it (success, snap, error). No continuous haptics.

### Gaps
- No global or mobile prevalence figure for users who turn on Reduce Motion or `prefers-reduced-motion` was found.
- NN/g's scrolljacking study N and device split were not visible.
- No measured study was found on spring vs bezier preference on mobile.

## 6. Mobile "AI slop" and template patterns, and what replaces them

### Takeaway
No independent study measures AI-generated mobile UI. Practitioner sources agree on a recognisable set of tells. On a phone these become **one long column of identical rounded cards** (icon-in-a-square, heading, two grey lines), a **centred pill badge + centred headline + purple/indigo gradient orb** hero, uniform radii and soft shadows on everything, a thin coloured left-border strip, Inter or system default type, bounce easing, and "elevate your workflow" copy. The documented fixes are process fixes: commit to tokens and one direction, ban the defaults explicitly, and default cards to borderless. The mobile-specific replacements below come from the evidence in sections 1–5.

### Cited Findings
- **[opinion]** Tells: a purple gradient orb behind the hero; cards with a thin grey border and soft shadow; a centred headline with a pill badge above it; three feature cards with the icon on top; six identical cards each with icon, heading and two lines; a 3–4 px coloured left-border strip, called "the single most reliable AI tell"; Inter plus an indigo-to-purple gradient; bounce easing on every hover; placeholder copy about "elevating your workflow". — [DEV, The Purple Gradient Problem](https://dev.to/james_anderson_h/the-purple-gradient-problem-why-ai-ui-all-looks-alike-and-how-to-fix-it-3j65); [925 Studios, AI slop tells](https://www.925studios.co/blog/ai-slop-design-tells); [SmoothUI, AI design slop](https://smoothui.dev/blog/ai-design-slop); [Mania Design, Spot the Slop](https://www.mania.design/blog/spot-the-slop-a-ui-designers-guide-to-fixing-ai-defaults/); [vibecodekit](https://vibecodekit.dev/ai-slop-design)
- **[opinion]** Cause: models drift to the statistical mean, and "the real cause of slop is no decision." Fixes: lock tokens up front (palette, type, spacing, radius), cap the palette, pick a real font pairing, default cards to borderless, check contrast, and run a scanner that flags `rounded-2xl shadow-lg` everywhere, reflexive glass and icon-in-rounded-square. — [avoid-ai-design (GitHub)](https://github.com/funboy322/avoid-ai-design); [rohitraj.tech, Hallmark](https://rohitraj.tech/notes/anti-ai-slop-design-skill-hallmark-guide-2026); [saascity](https://saascity.io/blog/stunning-frontend-designs-vibe-coding-avoid-ai-slop)
- **[consensus] Supporting mobile evidence for the replacements:** Apple HIG on alignment: "People assume that aligned items are related… indented items as subordinate". Use alignment and indentation for hierarchy rather than boxes. — [Apple HIG, Layout](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/layout). Material 3 Expressive: emphasise the key element by size, shape and colour, which made it up to 4× faster to find (company-measured). — [Dezeen](https://www.dezeen.com/2025/05/28/google-ushers-in-age-of-expressive-interfaces-with-material-design-update/amp/). Baymard: static content sections instead of carousels. — [Baymard](https://baymard.com/blog/homepage-carousel)

### Inferences
Mobile slop patterns and their replacements, each tied to the evidence above:

| Mobile slop pattern | Why it fails on a phone | Replace with |
|---|---|---|
| Stack of identical bordered/shadowed cards, each icon-square + heading + 2 grey lines | High element count with no hierarchy (complexity); every card has equal weight; wastes 390 px of width on padding and borders | **Unboxed list rows** aligned to one left edge, with dividers or spacing for grouping. **One** featured item at a larger size. Vary the content form (a number, an image, a quote), not just the text |
| Centred everything (hero, body, lists) | Centred multi-line text is slower to scan; on a narrow column it makes ragged left edges and has no anchor | **Left-aligned** text and a strong left edge. Centre only single short lines or a lone CTA |
| Pill eyebrow badge above a centred headline + gradient orb | Spends the most-viewed screen on decoration; prototypical of *AI*, not of the category | A headline that **is** the visual (large display type, tight leading), set against **one real image or product shot**, cropped full-bleed |
| Tiny grey secondary text (12–13 px, low contrast) | Fails legibility checks (Awwwards mobile criteria include font legibility and contrast); unreadable outdoors | Body ≥ 16 px; secondary text darker, not smaller; use weight or size to separate levels, not only grey |
| Auto-carousel of feature slides | Most slides are never seen; autorotation is bad on mobile (Baymard) | Static sections in scroll, or a **user-driven** horizontal rail with a visibly peeking next item |
| Hamburger-only nav on a product with 3–5 key sections | Hidden nav is less discoverable and slower (NN/g) | Visible bottom tab bar with labels (apps), or visible top-level links / bottom bar (web) |
| Fade-up-on-scroll on every block, bounce hovers | Ambient motion becomes noise (NN/g parallax); hover doesn't exist on touch | Motion only on user-caused state change; spring press feedback (scale ~0.97) on tap; one signature transition |
| Uniform 16–24 px radius + soft shadow on every surface | A default token value reads as template | Pick a radius **system**: e.g. sharp content and round controls, or one shape language that matches the brand. Use elevation only for things that actually float (sheets, bars) |

Rules for AI, the mobile slop check:
1. No more than one card style per screen, and a list of more than 3 same-shaped cards must become rows or gain a featured lead item.
2. Left-align by default. Centring is allowed only for a single line or a single button.
3. Minimum 16 px body text. Secondary text is distinguished by weight or colour at ≥ 4.5:1 contrast, never by going below 14 px.
4. The hero contains a real visual or a typographic statement, never a decorative gradient field. No eyebrow pill unless it carries real, time-bound information.
5. Distinctiveness lives in display type, one image or illustration language, one motion signature, and copy voice. Structure (navigation, scroll, back, tap targets) stays platform-typical.

### Gaps
- No empirical study was found that quantifies which mobile patterns users perceive as "AI-made" or "templated". All sources here are practitioner or vendor blogs, several of them selling their own anti-slop tools.
- No measured comparison was found of card grids versus unboxed lists on mobile for scanning speed or appeal.
